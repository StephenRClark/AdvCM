Example Class 4

In this example class we will consider some applications of Noether’s theorem.

As usual, we begin with a Multiple choice quiz.

Example exercise

1. Noether’s theorem for a multi-component field

In the lecture we derived it for a simple scalar field \(\varphi(x)\). Suppose instead of a single Lorentz scalar field we have a system described by a set of Lorentz scalar fields forming a multicomponent field \(\varphi_q(x)\), with \(q\) indexing over the components. Write down a plausible generalisation of Noether’s theorem for such a system.

Solution

1. Noether’s theorem for a multi-component field

For a multi-component Lorentz scalar field \(\varphi_q(x)\) we will have a multi-component symmetry induced variation \(\delta_S\varphi_q\). We still have a Lagrangian depending on \(\varphi_q\) and its derivatives \(\partial\varphi_q\), for all \(q\), so \[ \delta \mathcal{L}(x, \varphi_q, \partial\varphi_q, \delta_S\varphi_q) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \varphi_q}\delta_S\varphi_q + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi_q)}\partial_\mu \delta_S\varphi_q + \cdots \] Exactly what we had before, but now we must sum over the components \(q\). We could follow the whole derivation through again, but it is clear that if \[ \delta \mathcal{L} = \partial_\mu \mathcal{J}^\mu, \] then we get \[ j^\mu = \sum_q \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi_q)} \delta_S \varphi_q - \mathcal{J}^\mu, \] retaining an explicit summation over the \(q\) index.

Example exercise

2. Two-component real scalar field

Specialising to the case of two components \(\varphi_1(x)\) and \(\varphi_2(x)\) we have a system governed by a Lagrangian density \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm two} = \partial_\mu \varphi_1\partial^\mu\varphi_1 + \partial_\mu \varphi_2\partial^\mu\varphi_2. \] Consider “rotations” by an angle \(\tau\) within the internal 2D space of these field components as: \[ R_\tau : \left\{\begin{array}{cccc} \varphi_1 & \rightarrow & \cos(\tau)\varphi_1 + \sin(\tau)\varphi_2 &= \bar\varphi_1 \\ \varphi_2 & \rightarrow & -\sin(\tau)\varphi_1 + \cos(\tau)\varphi_2 &= \bar\varphi_2 \end{array}\right. \] Show explicitly that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm two}\) is invariant under this transformation. Compute the infinitesimal variations to the fields \(\delta_S\varphi_q\) from this transformations and compute the corresponding Noether current for this symmetry. Verify that the current is 4-divergence-free.

Solution

2. Two-component real scalar field

Specialise to a two-component field \(\phi_1(x)\) and \(\phi_2(x)\) governed by a Lagrangian \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm two} = \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_1 + \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_2. \] This is the sum of two separate wave-equation (d’Alembert) Lagrangians we have encountered before. Now we rotate these components by an angle \(\alpha\): \[ \begin{align} \phi_1 &\rightarrow \cos\alpha \, \phi_1 + \sin\alpha \, \phi_2, \\ \phi_2 &\rightarrow -\sin\alpha \, \phi_1 + \cos\alpha \, \phi_2. \end{align} \] So \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{two}}\) after this rotation is: \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}'_{\text{two}} &= \partial_\mu(\cos\alpha \phi_1 + \sin\alpha \phi_2)\partial^\mu(\cos\alpha \phi_1 + \sin\alpha \phi_2) \\ &\quad + \partial_\mu(-\sin\alpha \phi_1 + \cos\alpha \phi_2)\partial^\mu(-\sin\alpha \phi_1 + \cos\alpha \phi_2). \end{align} \] Expand: \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}'_{\text{two}} &= \cos^2\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_1 + \cos\alpha \sin\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_2 \\ &\quad + \sin^2\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_2 + \sin\alpha \cos\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_1 \\ &\quad + \sin^2\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_1 - \sin\alpha \cos\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_2 \\ &\quad + \cos^2\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_2 - \cos\alpha \sin\alpha \, \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_1, \\ &= \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_1 + \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_2, \\ &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{two}}, \end{align} \] So \(\delta \mathcal{L} = 0\) since the Lagrangian is invariant.

Now consider infinitesimal transformations: \[ \delta \phi_1 = \alpha \phi_2 + \dots, \qquad \delta \phi_2 = -\alpha \phi_1 + \dots. \] The Noether current in this context is \[ j^\mu = \sum_q \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \phi_q)} \delta \phi_q. \] so using \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \phi_q)} = 2 \partial^\mu \phi_q, \] we obtain \[ j^\mu = 2 \partial^\mu \phi_1 (\alpha \phi_2) + 2 \partial^\mu \phi_2 (-\alpha \phi_1). \] Hence \[ j^\mu = 2\alpha \left( \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_1 - \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_2 \right). \] The scale factors of 2 and \(\alpha\) are irrelevant, so we can drop them. We then verify the 4-current has zero 4-divergence: \[ \partial_\mu j^\mu = \partial_\mu \phi_2 \partial^\mu \phi_1 + \phi_2 \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_1 - \partial_\mu \phi_1 \partial^\mu \phi_2 - \phi_1 \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_2. \] The first and third terms cancel, leaving \[ \partial_\mu j^\mu = \phi_2 \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_1 - \phi_1 \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_2. \] Using the Euler–Lagrange equations \[ \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_1 = 0, \qquad \partial_\mu \partial^\mu \phi_2 = 0, \] we then obtain \[ \partial_\mu j^\mu = 0, \] so \(j^\mu\) does indeed represent a conserved quantity.

Example exercise

3. A complex scalar field

Now suppose that instead of a two-component Lorentz scalar field we have a single complex Lorentz scalar field \(\varphi = \varphi_1 + {\rm i}\varphi_2\). Explain why we can treat \(\varphi\) and its complex conjugate \(\varphi^*\) as independent fields. Show that the following Lagrangian density \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm complex} = \partial_\mu \varphi\partial^\mu\varphi^* - m^2\varphi^*\varphi, \] is invariant to a phase shift of the field \(\varphi \rightarrow e^{{\rm i}\alpha}\varphi\), with \(\alpha\) a real constant, find its Noether current, and shows it is 4-divergence-free.

Solution

3. A complex scalar field

If \(\varphi(x)\) is complex then it is really a two component scalar field: \[ \varphi(x) = \varphi_1(x) + i\varphi_2(x) \] with independent real and imaginary parts. We can equally perform a change of basis by \(\pi/4\) in the Argand plane and take: \[ \varphi(x) = \varphi_1(x) + i\varphi_2(x), \qquad \varphi^*(x) = \varphi_1(x) - i\varphi_2(x). \] so we can also treat the field and its complex conjugate independent. We can then construct a complex Lagrangian as given \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{complex}} = \partial_\mu \varphi \partial^\mu \varphi^* - m^2 \varphi^* \varphi, \] which includes a usual “wave” term along with a new “mass” term. Notice that \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{complex}}\) is real-valued Lorentz scalar, as it must be. If we transform the “wave” term back into the real and imaginary parts we find that \[ \begin{align} \partial_\mu \varphi \partial^\mu \varphi^* &= \partial_\mu(\varphi_1 + i\varphi_2)\partial^\mu(\varphi_1 - i\varphi_2), \\ &= \partial_\mu \varphi_1 \partial^\mu \varphi_1 + \partial_\mu \varphi_2 \partial^\mu \varphi_2 + \text{cross terms cancel}, \\ &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{two}}, \end{align} \] so this part of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{complex}}\) is actually identical to the two component field problem in 2.. The “mass” term however changes the physics of this problem, as we will explore at the very end of this course.

The Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{complex}}\) is clearly invariant under \[ \varphi \rightarrow e^{i\alpha}\varphi, \qquad \varphi^* \rightarrow e^{-i\alpha}\varphi^* \] with \(\alpha\) real. So \(\delta \mathcal{L} = 0\). Examining the infinitesimal we find to lowest order: \[ \delta \varphi = i\alpha \varphi + \dots, \qquad \delta \varphi^* = -i\alpha \varphi^* + \dots \] so using \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)} = \partial^\mu \varphi^* \] and \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi^*)} = \partial^\mu \varphi \] the Noether current \[ j^\mu_{\text{complex}} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\delta \varphi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi^*)}\delta \varphi^*. \] evaluates as \[ j^\mu_{\text{complex}} = i\alpha(\varphi \partial^\mu \varphi^* - \varphi^* \partial^\mu \varphi). \] Again we can drop the scale factor \(\alpha\) and just use \[ j^\mu_{\text{complex}} = i(\varphi \partial^\mu \varphi^* - \varphi^* \partial^\mu \varphi) \] Again we check it is 4-divergence free and get \[ \begin{align} \partial_\mu j^\mu_{\text{complex}} &= i(\partial_\mu \varphi \partial^\mu \varphi^* + \varphi \partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi^* - \partial_\mu \varphi^* \partial^\mu \varphi - \varphi^* \partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi), \\ &= i( \varphi \partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi^* - \varphi^* \partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi) \end{align} \] after cancelling terms. Then by using the Euler-Lagrange equation for this theory \[ (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\varphi = 0, \] and its conjugate \[ (\partial_\mu\partial^\mu + m^2)\varphi^* = 0, \] we see that \[ \partial_\mu j^\mu_{\text{complex}} = i( \varphi (-m\varphi^*) - \varphi^* (-m\varphi)) = 0 \] as required.

Example exercise

4. Verifying the stress-energy tensor

In the lectures we considered Lagrangian densities \(\mathcal{L}(\varphi,\partial\varphi)\) without any explicit space-time dependence and showed that they possess a symmetry for space-time translations \(x^\nu \rightarrow x^\nu - a^\nu\) performed on the \(\varphi\). For each translation direction \(\nu=0,1,2,3,\) we obtained a 4-current described by the rank-2 stress-energy tensor: \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial (\partial_{\mu} \varphi)} \partial^{\nu} \varphi - \mathcal{L} \eta^{\mu\nu}. \] Show in general that each of these currents are 4-divergence free \(\partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0\).

Solution

4. Verifying the stress-energy tensor

In this question we are working backwards from our derivation of the stress-energy tensor. We start with the stress-energy tensor of a scalar field: \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\partial^\nu \varphi - \eta^{\mu\nu}\mathcal{L}, \] and then compute \[ \begin{align} \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} &= \partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\partial^\nu \varphi\right) - \partial^\nu \mathcal{L}, \\ &= \partial_\mu\left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\right)\partial^\nu \varphi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\partial_\mu\partial^\nu \varphi - \partial^\nu \mathcal{L}, \\ &= \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi}\partial^\nu \varphi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\partial_\mu\partial^\nu \varphi - \partial^\nu \mathcal{L}, \end{align} \] where in the final step we have used the Euler-Lagrange equations. Now we use that the Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}(\varphi,\partial\varphi)\) is a function only of the field and its derivative, so has no explicit dependence on \(x\). Consequently, the “total” partial derivative of the Lagrangian follows as \[ \partial^\nu \mathcal{L} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi}\partial^\nu \varphi + \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu \varphi)}\partial_\mu\partial^\nu \varphi, \] hence, it cancels the first two terms giving \[ \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = 0. \] The importance of \(T^{\mu\nu}\) stems from it being the source term for the gravitational field in general relativity, corresponding to the curvature of spacetime.
