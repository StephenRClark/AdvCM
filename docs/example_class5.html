Example Class 5

In this example class we will explore what happens if we add a “mass” term to Maxwell’s theory.

Let’s do our final Multiple choice quiz.

Example exercise

1. Adding a mass term to a scalar field

In the lectures we have considered a Lorentz real scalar field \(\varphi(x)\) governed by the d’Alembert Lagrangian \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{d'A}} = -\partial_{\mu}\varphi \partial^{\mu}\varphi. \]

  1. Confirm Euler-Lagrange equation resulting from \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{d'A}}\) is the wave-equation \(\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\varphi(x) = 0\) and show that plane-waves \(\varphi(x) = ae^{ik_\mu x^\mu}\) are solutions so long as their 4-wave-vector \(\vec{K}\) is null, i.e. \(k_\mu k^\mu = 0\). What is the wave’s dispersion relation and what does it imply about it?

Now consider the Klein-Gordon Lagrangian \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{d'A}} + \frac{m^2 c^2}{\hbar^2} \varphi^2, \] where a “mass” term has been added, motivated by the Einstein-Planck relation \(\mathcal{E} = \hbar\omega\) and the de-Broglie relation \(\boldsymbol{p} = \hbar\boldsymbol{k}\).

  1. Assuming the action \(\mathcal{S}\) has the same units as \(\hbar\) what units does a scalar field \(\varphi(x)\) need to have? Given the “mass” term is proportional to \(\varphi^2\) use dimensional analysis involving the Lorentz scalars \(m\), \(c\) and \(\hbar\) to show its coefficients must be those used in \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}}\).

  2. What is Euler-Lagrange equation resulting from \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}}\) and what is the dispersion relation for plane-waves? Why is this said to describe a “massive” scalar field?

Solution

1. Adding a mass term to a scalar field

  1. We use the relativistic Euler-Lagrange equation \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\varphi} - \partial_\mu\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial\partial_\mu\varphi} = 0, \] noting immediately that in our case \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{d'A}}}{\partial\varphi} = 0, \] and from the lecture Building a field theory we know that \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{d'A}}}{\partial \partial_\mu \phi} = -2 \eta^{\mu\nu} \partial_\nu \varphi = -2\partial^\mu\varphi, \] hence the equation of motion is \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\varphi(x) = 0, \] which is the wave-equation. As described in Plane-waves and particle flow we then insert (for convenience) a complex plane-wave \(\varphi(x) = ae^{ik_\mu x^\mu}\) and compute \[ \partial_\mu e^{i k_\nu x^\nu} = ik_\mu e^{i k_\sigma x^\sigma}, \] so \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi(x) = (ik^\mu)(ik_\mu)\varphi(x) = -k_\mu k^\mu \varphi(x) = 0. \] Given the plane-wave \(\varphi(x) \neq 0\) unless its amplitude \(a = 0\) it can only be a general solution to the wave-equation if \(k_\mu k^\mu = 0\). Hence the 4-wave-vector must be a lightlike 4-vector. This implies \[ k_\mu k^\mu = \left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^2 - \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{k} = \left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^2 - |\boldsymbol{k}|^2 = 0, \] or more compactly \[ \omega = \pm c|\boldsymbol{k}|. \] From this we see that the wave has a phase velocity \[ v_p = \omega/k = \pm c, \] where \(k = |\boldsymbol{k}|\), and a group velocity \[ v_g = \frac{{\rm d}\omega}{{\rm d}k} = \pm c. \] This theory describes a dispersionless wave travelling at the speed of light.

  2. To fix the dimensions of a scalar field \(\varphi(x)\) we work backwards from the action which is taken as having the units of \([{\tt energy}][{\tt time}]\), the same as the units of \(\hbar\). So since \[ \mathcal{S} = \int_\Omega {\rm d}^4x \mathcal{L}(x), \] since \([{\rm d}^4x] = [{\tt length}]^4\) then we infer that the Lagrangian (density) \[ [\mathcal{L}] = \frac{[{\tt energy}][{\tt time}]}{[{\tt length}]^{4}} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{[{\tt energy}]}{[{\tt length}]^{3}}, \] so, up to a factor of \(c^{-1}\) has units of energy density. Examining the kinetic term we get a dimension of inverse length per derivative so \[ [\partial_{\mu}\varphi \partial^{\mu}\varphi] = [{\tt length}^{-2}][\varphi]^2 = [\mathcal{L}], \] hence we get \[ [\varphi] = \sqrt{\frac{[{\tt energy}][{\tt time}]}{[{\tt length}]^2}} \] Now if we take the coefficient of the mass term to be \(\alpha\) then \[ \left[\alpha\varphi^2\right] = [\alpha]^2\frac{[{\tt energy}][{\tt time}]}{[{\tt length}]^2} = [\mathcal{L}], \] hence \([\alpha] = [{\tt length}]^{-2}\). Next, we solve \[ [m^g c^h \hbar^q] = [{\tt length}]^{-2}, \] by expanding the dimensions as \[ [{\tt mass}]^g [{\tt length}]^h [{\tt time}]^{-h} [{\tt mass}]^q [{\tt length}]^{2q} [{\tt time}]^{-q} = [{\tt length}]^{-2}, \] from which we see that \(g = -q\), \(h = -q\) and \(h + 2q = -2\), so \(q = -2\), \(h = 2\) and \(g = 2\) giving the coefficient of the “mass” term as \[ \left[\frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\right] = \frac{[{\tt mass}]^2 [{\tt length}]^2 [{\tt time}]^{-2}}{[{\tt mass}]^2[{\tt length}]^4[{\tt time}]^{-2}} = [{\tt length}]^{-2}. \]

  3. To find the equation of motion for the Klein-Gordon theory we need only compute one additional term \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}}}{\partial\varphi} = \frac{2m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\varphi, \] with the derivative term is identical to the d’Alembert case \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KG}}}{\partial \partial_\mu \phi} = -2 \eta^{\mu\nu} \partial_\nu \varphi = -2\partial^\mu\varphi, \] so the relativistic Euler-Lagrange equation gives \[ 2\frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\varphi(x) - (-2) \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\varphi(x) = 0, \] so \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu\varphi(x) + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\varphi(x) = 0, \] hence we acquire an additional term to the standard wave-equation. Inserting a plane-wave we can use the result from the first part to get \[ \left(-k_\mu k^\mu + \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2}\right)\varphi(x) = 0, \] hence \[ \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2} - k_\mu k^\mu = 0. \] Expanding out \[ \frac{m^2c^2}{\hbar^2} - \left(\frac{\omega}{c}\right)^2 + \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{k} = 0 \] and rearranging \[ (\hbar\omega)^2 = (\hbar k)^2c^2 + m^2c^4. \] If we insert the Einstein-Planck and de-Broglie relations we recover \[ \mathcal{E}^2 = p^2c^2 + m^2c^4, \] or in otherwords the relativistic energy-momentum relation for a particle of mass \(m\). For this reason the term we added, with its delibrate factor of \(\hbar^2\) and \(c^2\) is called a “mass” term.

Example exercise

2. Adding a mass term to EM

Let us now investigate what happens if we add a similar term to the electromagnetic Lagrangian, giving us the \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EM}} + \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0} A_{\rho} A^{\rho} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} (\partial_{\mu} A_{\nu} - \partial_{\nu} A_{\mu})(\partial^{\mu} A^{\nu} - \partial^{\nu} A^{\mu}) + \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0} A_{\rho} A^{\rho}. \] where \(A^{\mu}\) is a real-valued 4-vector “Proca potential”. In the limit \(\mu \to 0\) the Proca theory reduces to conventional electromagnetism. By analogy with regular electromagnetism, the generalized curl of the Proca potential gives the Proca field tensor \[ F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_{\mu} A_{\nu} - \partial_{\nu} A_{\mu}, \] and the Proca Lagrangian describing interaction with a 4-current density \(j^{\mu}\) is \[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca-int}} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca}} + \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0} F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+ \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0} A_{\rho} A^{\rho} - A_{\sigma}J^{\sigma}. \]

  1. Find the physical dimension of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{int}},\) and check they agree. What dimension should \(\mu\) in the Proca Lagrangian have? By analogy with the Klein-Gordon equation, what physical quantity does this suggest a Proca field has, which an electromagnetic field doesn’t?

  2. Identify a family of symmetries of \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{EM}}\) not shared by \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca}}\). What do you conclude about the “Proca potential” \(A_{\nu}\)?

  3. Use the relativistic Euler-Lagrange equation for \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca}}\) with respect to \(A_{\mu}\) to show the “homogeneous Proca equation” is \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \mu^2A^\nu = 0. \tag{1}\] What has happened to the Proca counterpart of the other relativistic Maxwell equation, i.e. Bianchi identity?

Solution

  1. Let’s start with the dimensions of force \(\boldsymbol{F}\) are \[ [\boldsymbol{F}] = [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}]^{-2}. \] From the Lorentz force equation we know \[ [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}]^{-2} = q(\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{B}) = [{\tt charge}] [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}]^{-1} [\boldsymbol{B}], \] so the magnetic field has units \[ [\boldsymbol{B}] = [{\tt charge}]^{-1} [{\tt mass}] [{\tt time}]^{-1} \] So since the the 3-vector potential \(\boldsymbol{B} = \nabla \times \boldsymbol{A}\), then \[ [\boldsymbol{A}] = [{\tt charge}]^{-1} [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}]^{-1}, \] which is also the dimensions of the 4-potential \(A^\mu\). The 3-current density \[ [\boldsymbol{j}] = [\rho \boldsymbol{v}] = [{\tt charge}] [{\tt length}]^{-3} \cdot [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}]^{-1} = [{\tt charge}] [{\tt length}] ^{-2} [{\tt time}] ^{-1}. \] Thus the interaction term has units \[ \begin{align} [\mathcal{L}_{\text{int}}] &= [A_\mu J^\mu] = [{\tt charge}]^{-1} [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}] [{\tt time}] ^{-2} \cdot [{\tt charge}] [{\tt length}]^{-2} [{\tt time}]^{-1}\\ &= [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}]^{-1} [{\tt time}]^{-2}. \end{align} \] These are the dimensions of energy density, since \[ [\text{energy density}] = [{\tt energy}] [{\tt length}]^{-3} = [{\tt mass}] [{\tt length}]^{-1} [{\tt time}]^{-2}. \] This is the same units as \[ [\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}] = \left[\frac{B^2}{\mu_0}\right], \] justified by the expression for the energy density of the EM field. Clearly, by inspection of \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}\), \[ \left[\frac{(\partial_\mu A_\nu)^2}{\mu_0}\right] = \left[\frac{\mu^2 A^2}{\mu_0}\right], \] meaning that the dimensions of the Proca parameter is \[ [\mu] = [{\tt length}]^{-1}, \] an inverse length. Thus including an inverse length squared to a term with the field squared is reminiscent of adding \(m^2c^2/\hbar^2\) to the Klein–Gordon Lagrangian density in 1. to get the KG Lagrangian. We therefore postulate that the Proca Lagrangian corresponds to a massive field with mass \(m\), related to \(\mu\) by \[ \mu = \frac{m\hbar}{c}. \] Clearly, an EM field in Maxwell’s theory has no corresponding mass.

  2. For EM, the Lagrangian is invariant under gauge transformations \[ A_\mu \rightarrow \bar{A}_\mu = A_\mu - \partial_\mu \chi \] for some Lorentz scalar field \(\chi\). And so \[ \delta \mathcal{L}_{EM} = 0, \] under this transformation. However, \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}} = \mathcal{L}_{EM} + \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0} A_\mu A^\mu, \] so under the same transformation,
    \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}} &\rightarrow \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Proca}} = \mathcal{L}_{EM} + \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0}(A_\mu - \partial_\mu \chi)(A^\mu - \partial^\mu \chi), \\ &= \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}} + \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0}(-2 \partial_\mu \chi A^\mu + \partial_\mu \chi \partial^\mu \chi). \end{align} \] So under the transformation, \[ \bar{\mathcal{L}}_{\text{Proca}} \neq \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}. \] Hence the Proca Lagrangian is not invariant under gauge symmetry. We conclude that the “Proca potential” is physically fixed without gauge symmetry.

  3. The EL equation for the Proca Lagrangian is \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}}{\partial A_\nu} - \partial_\mu \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)} = 0. \] Now, \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}}{\partial A_\nu} = \frac{\mu^2}{2\mu_0} \frac{\partial (A_\mu A^\mu)}{\partial A_\nu} = \frac{\mu^2}{\mu_0} A^\nu. \] Also, \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\text{Proca}}}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{EM}}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\nu)} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0} F^{\mu\nu}. \] Thus \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \mu^2 A^\nu = 0. \] This is the homogeneous Proca equation, equivalent to the source-free Maxwell equation \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0. \] The other Maxwell equation, the Bianchi identity, \[ \partial_\nu F_{\mu\rho} + \partial_\mu F_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\rho F_{\nu\mu} = 0, \] also automatically holds for the Proca field tensor since it was defined as \(F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_{\mu} A_{\nu} - \partial_{\nu} A_{\mu}\).

Example exercise

3. Properties of the Proca equation

We now explore some implications of the structure of the Proca equation derived in 2..

  1. Show that if a 4-vector field \(A_{\mu}\) satisfies the homogeneous Proca equation in Equation 1, then it satisfies the Lorenz gauge condition. Use this to show that each component of the Proca \(A_{\mu}\) field satisfies the Klein-Gordon equation.

  2. Write down a complex plane wave solution of the Proca equation. Does its 4-wavevector have the properties you expect? What about the polarization of the analogue of \(\boldsymbol{E}\) in the field tensor?

  3. Now consider the “inhomogeneous Proca equation” by following the same procedure as the previous question, but with \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{Proca-int}}.\) Prove that the electromagnetic 4-current is divergenceless from the relativistic inhomogeneous Maxwell equation. Is the 4-current source \(j^{\mu}\) in the Proca equation divergenceless?

Solution

3. Properties of the Proca equation

  1. From the homogeneous Proca equation in Equation 1 we have \[ \partial_\nu A^\nu = -\frac{1}{\mu^2} \partial_\nu \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu}. \] Now by antisymmetry, \[ \partial_\nu \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = -\partial_\mu \partial_\nu F^{\mu\nu} \] so the symmetry of the double derivative means \[ \partial_\nu \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0. \] Thus the Proca potential automatically obeys the Lorenz gauage condition \[ \partial_\mu A^\mu = 0, \] but no gauge fixing had to be made. Now consider the Proca equation depending on \(A^\nu\) has the form: \[ \partial_\mu(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu) + \mu^2 A^\nu = 0 \] so \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) + \mu^2 A^\nu = 0. \] Using Lorenz condition, \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu + \mu^2 A^\nu = 0, \] which is the Klein–Gordon equation for each component.

  2. A complex plane wave solution of the Proca equation has the form \[ A^\nu = A^{(0)\nu} e^{-i k_\mu x^\mu}, \] for constant polarisation 4-vector \(A^{(0)\nu}\). Substituting this into Proca equation gives \[ (-k_\mu k^\mu + \mu^2) A^{(0)\nu} = 0, \] thus \[ k_\mu k^\mu = \mu^2, \] exactly as we had in 1. for a massive scalar field. This is rewritten as \[ \frac{\omega^2}{c^2} - |\boldsymbol{k}|^2 = \mu^2. \] and again identifying \[ E = \hbar \omega, \qquad \boldsymbol{p} = \hbar \boldsymbol{k}, \] we get \[ E^2 = m^2 c^4 + |\boldsymbol{p}|^2 c^2, \] as expected for a massive relativistic particle. Now for the plane wave, the Proca field tensor is \[ F^{\mu\nu} = F^{(0)\mu\nu}e^{-i k_\sigma x^\sigma}, \] for a constant field tensor \[ F^{(0)\mu\nu} = -i(k^\mu A^{(0)\nu} - k^\nu A^{(0)\mu}). \] So for a plane-wave the Proca equation becomes \[ -i k_\mu F^{(0)\mu\nu} = \mu^2 A^{(0)\nu}, \] and given the Proca analogous of the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields we have \[ F^{(0)10} = E^{(0)}_x/c, \quad F^{(0)20} = E^{(0)}_y/c, \quad F^{(0)30} = E^{(0)}_z/c \] we can evaluate \[ -i k_\mu F^{(0)\mu 0} = -i (-\boldsymbol{k})\cdot(+\boldsymbol{E}) = -\mu^2 A^{(0)0}, \] so we find that \[ \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{E} = i\mu^2 A^{(0)0}, \] meaning the Proca \(\boldsymbol{E}\) is not orthogonal to the 3-wave-vector \(\boldsymbol{k}\) of the plane-wave indicating the Proca wave it is not a transverse wave.

  3. Working with \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm Proca-int}\) the argument from the Euler-Lagrange equation is identical, except in the inhomogeneous case we have \[ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{\rm Proca-int}}{\partial A_\nu} = \frac{\mu^2}{\mu_0}A^\nu - j^\nu \] which gives the inhomogeneous Proca equation \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \mu^2 A^\nu = \mu_0 j^\nu. \] As expected this reduces to the usual relativistic Maxwell equation when the limit \(\mu \to 0\) is taken. In this limit, taking divergence gives \[ \partial_\nu j^\nu = \mu_0^{-1}\partial_\nu \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0, \] for the same reasons as in part (a), so the current is 4-divergence free. For the inhomogeneous Proca equation we have instead \[ \partial_\nu j^\nu = \mu_0^{-1}(\partial_\nu \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \mu^2 \partial_\nu A^\nu). \] Again the first term vanishes on the righthand side vanishes, so \[ \partial_\nu j^\nu = \mu_0^{-1} \mu^2 \partial_\nu A^\nu. \] Thus current conservation holds only if Lorenz condition is satisfied for the Proca potential. Although we discussed the \(\partial_\nu A^\nu = 0\) condition being satisfied by \(A^\nu\) in part (a), we only proved it for the homogeneous case.

Example exercise

4. Proca electrostatics

Fix a frame of reference, and write down the Proca analogues of the four Maxwell equations. In our frame of reference, assume that we are in the “Proca electrostatic” case, with a stationary point charge \(q\) at the origin. Prove that a solution for the Proca scalar potential in this case is given by \[ V(r) = q\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\mu r}}{4\pi \varepsilon_{0} r}, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2+y^2+z^2} > 0. \] Assume spherical symmetry where you may find the formula for the radial Laplacian \[ \nabla^2 = \partial_r^2 + \frac{2}{r}\partial_r, \] useful. How does this compare to the corresponding EM situation? If photons were to have mass, what elementary physical law of electromagnetism would have to be modified? Comment on the consequences of this.

Solution

4. Proca electrostatics

Since the Proca field tensor obeys the Bianchi identity \[ \partial_\nu F_{\mu\rho} + \partial_\mu F_{\rho\nu} + \partial_\rho F_{\nu\mu} = 0, \] we know that its analogues of the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields obey Maxwell’s equations (M1) and (M2) \[ \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{B} = 0, \quad \nabla \times \boldsymbol{E} + \dot{\boldsymbol{B}} = 0. \] Given the inhomogeneous equation \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} + \mu^2 A^\nu = \mu_0 j^\nu, \] and using \[ A^\nu = (V/c, \boldsymbol{A}), \qquad J^\nu = (\rho c, \boldsymbol{J}), \] we obtain from the 0-component: \[ \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{E} + \mu^2 V = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \] and from the \(i=1,2,3\) components: \[ \nabla \times \boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2} \frac{\partial \boldsymbol{E}}{\partial t} + \mu^2 \boldsymbol{A} = \mu_0 \boldsymbol{J}. \] If we are in Proca electrostatics then we expect that the electric field obeys \[ \boldsymbol{E} = -\nabla V, \] from \[ E^j/c = \partial^j A^0 - \partial^0 A^j = -\partial_j V/c. \] Thus we have \[ -\nabla^2 V + \mu^2 V = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \] but if the charge is at the origin then for \(r>0\) \[ \nabla^2 V = \mu^2 V. \] Using spherical symmetry: \[ \nabla^2 = \frac{d^2}{dr^2} + \frac{2}{r}\frac{d}{dr}, \] for functions with only radial dependence, the radial equation for the Proca scalar potential \(V(r)\) is this ODE for \(r>0\) \[ r V''(r) + 2 V'(r) = \mu^2 r V(r). \] We now check the suggested form \[ V(r) = \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r} e^{-\mu r}, \] by computing derivatives \[ V'(r) = -\frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r^2} e^{-\mu r} - \mu \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r} e^{-\mu r} = \left(-\mu -\frac{1}{r}\right)V(r) \] and \[ V'' = -\mu\left(-\mu -\frac{1}{r}\right)V(r) + \frac{\mu}{r}V(r) + 2\frac{V(r)}{r^2} = \left(\mu^2 + \frac{2\mu}{r} + \frac{2}{r^2}\right)V(r). \] Substitute back in \[ r V'' + 2 V' = f\left(\mu^2 + \frac{2\mu}{r} + \frac{2}{r^2}\right)V(r)+ 2\left(-\mu -\frac{1}{r}\right)V(r) = \mu^2 r V(r), \] showing the solution holds. In the EM limit \(\mu \to 0\):
\[ V \rightarrow \frac{q}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r}, \] which is the familiar Coulomb potential. If photons had mass, the Coulomb force would need to be modified. Rather than following an inverse square law, the “massive EM” \(\boldsymbol{E}\) field would fall off exponentially characteristic of Yukawa type potential: \[ V \sim \frac{e^{-\mu r}}{r}. \] This implies that forces due to massive fields fall off rapidly and only being significant within the Compton wavelength of the charge. For photon mass comparable to electron the range would be \[ \sim 10^{-12} \text{ m}, \] or about 1% of a Bohr radii, and is thus very short range. This is why it is hard to observe the effects of massive spin-1 particles.

The Proca equation is “the” relativistic wave equation for a massive, spin-1 particle, and so provides the (non-quantized) field theory for vector bosons, such as the mediating particles of the nuclear forces (\(W^{\pm},Z\) for the weak force, and gluons for the strong force) and some mesons (which are composite particles, but different from protons and neutrons). Further details of the physics of these particles is determined by interaction terms, which determine the coupling to other fields (both electromagnetic, and other kinds of interactions). Note that a Proca field/particle as considered here is not itself electrically charged.
