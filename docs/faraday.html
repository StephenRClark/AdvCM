12  Faraday tensor and relativistic Maxwell equations

In this lecture we will construct the Faraday tensor, the central object for describing electromagnetism, and we will reformulate Maxwell’s equation, reviewed in the last lecture, into an elegant relativistically covariant form.

12.1 Justifying the Faraday tensor

In Maxwell’s theory of electromagnetism is described in terms of the electric and magnetic 3-vector fields in space-time: \[ \boldsymbol{E}(t,\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{pmatrix} E_x(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \\ E_y(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \\ E_z(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \end{pmatrix}, \quad {\rm and} \quad \boldsymbol{B}(t,\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{pmatrix} B_x(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \\ B_y(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \\ B_z(t,\boldsymbol{r}) \end{pmatrix}. \tag{12.1}\]

We now want to package this same information into a relativistic formalism. It is reasonable to expect we should be able to do this with Maxwell’s theory unmodified since electromagnetism is the original relativtistic field theory. Recall what covariant objects we have at our disposal in which we need to package the six EM field components from Equation 12.1:

After all, in the first lecture we used the lack of Galilean covariance of the wave equation for light to motivate the light-speed postulate that constructed special relativity in the first place.
  • Lorentz scalars - we would need six of them, but we know that the field components are not the same in all frames of reference. \(\boldsymbol{\times}\)
  • 4-vectors - these have four components, so one is not enough, but two is too many, and using one plus two Lorentz scalars makes no sense as it would not treat all the components on the same footing. \(\boldsymbol{\times}\)
  • Tensors - We now have much more components to play with. The simplest possible is a rank-2 tensor which we will denote as \(F^{\mu\nu}\). We have several options:
    • general tensor - In this case all sixteen components of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) are independent so it is too large. \(\boldsymbol{\times}\)
    • symmetric tensor - This means \(F^{\mu\nu} = F^{\nu \mu}\), but still has ten independent components so is also too large. \(\boldsymbol{\times}\)
    • antisymmetric tensor - We now have \(F^{\mu\nu} = -F^{\nu\mu}\) giving six independent elements. This looks very promising as a container for two 3-vectors! \(\boldsymbol{\checkmark}\)

An antisymmetric tensor will have a matrix representation \(F^{\mu\nu} = [\boldsymbol{F}]_{\mu\nu}\) of the form \[ \boldsymbol{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \circ & \circ & \circ \\ - & 0 & \circ & \circ \\ - & - & 0 & \circ \\ - & - & - & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \] where the elements in the lower triangle are the negation \(-\) of the elements \(\circ\) in the upper triangle. We will focus on partitioning this matrix distinguishing time from space as: \[ \boldsymbol{F} = \left(\begin{array}{clcr} \square & \sqsubset & & \sqsupset \\ \sqcap & \ulcorner & & \urcorner \\ & & & \\ \sqcup & \llcorner & & \lrcorner \end{array}\right), \tag{12.2}\] so we have a 3-vector in the spatial elements of the first column, and a spatial \(3\times 3\) sub-matrix which is itself antisymmetric. We can therefore insert one 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{a}\) into this tensor directly as \[ \boldsymbol{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -a_x & -a_y & -a_z \\ a_x & 0 & \circ & \circ \\ a_y & - & 0 & \circ \\ a_z & - & - & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] or in more compact block matrix notation according to our partitioning \[ \boldsymbol{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{a} & \boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{12.3}\] where \(\boldsymbol{A}\) is \(3\times 3\) antisymmetric matrix. But why do we use this paritioning and not another form? The reason for this is based on how a rank-2 tensor transforms under rotations. Maxwell’s theory tells us that spatial rotations do not mix \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields - we can’t turn a magnetic field in to an electric field just by orientating a magnet along another axis. We must identify 3-vectors in a rank-2 tensor which do not mix under spatial rotation.

Recall that the transformation properties of a type \(({2 \atop 0})\)-tensor are \[ F^{{\bar \mu}{\bar \nu}} = \Lambda^{\bar \mu}_\sigma \Lambda^{\bar \nu}_\tau F^{\sigma\tau}. \] Representing the Lorentz transform as a matrix \([{\boldsymbol\Lambda}]_{\bar{\mu}\sigma} = \Lambda_\sigma^{\bar\mu}\) then, as we have seen several times now, the matrix version of this transformation becomes \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{F}} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{\rm T}. \] As we saw in Introducing 4-vectors rotations are a special case of Lorentz transformation with the form: \[ \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R} = \left(\begin{array}{cccc} 1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \ulcorner & & \urcorner \\ 0 & & \mathbf{R} & \\ 0 & \llcorner & & \lrcorner \end{array}\right). \tag{12.4}\] Crucially we see that \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R}\) has exactly the same block structure as Equation 12.2. It is easy to confirm that this partitioning is preserved under spatial rotations, meaning the components the 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{a}\) and the antisymmetric matrix \(\boldsymbol{A}\) are never mixed, as you are invited to show.

Work-along exercises

1. Partitioning under rotation

Use the block matrix form in Equation 12.2 to show that when a rotation \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R}\) in Equation 12.4 is applied to \(\boldsymbol{F}\) in Equation 12.3 none of the blocks are mixed in the resulting matrix \(\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\).

Solution

1. Partitioning under rotation

To show this we simply carry out the block matrix multiplication \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{F}} = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \mathbf{R} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{a} & \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{b}) \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \mathbf{R}^{\rm T} \end{pmatrix}. \] This proceed with exactly the same “row-on-column” operation as normal matrix multiplication, but now we get scalar, row-vectors, column-vectors and matrices as outputs from the operations. To see this let’s do the first multiplication \[ \begin{pmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \mathbf{R} \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{a} & \boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1\cdot0 + \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{a} & -1\cdot\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} + \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R} \\ 0\cdot\boldsymbol{0} + \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a} & -\boldsymbol{0}\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} + \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a} & \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}. \] The second multiplication follows similarly as \[ \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a} & \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \boldsymbol{0}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{0} & \mathbf{R}^{\rm T} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T}\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} \\ \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a} & \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} \end{pmatrix} = \bar{\boldsymbol{F}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\rm T} \\ \bar{\boldsymbol{a}} & \bar{\boldsymbol{A}} \end{pmatrix}. \] From this we can immediately identify \(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a}\), and that \(\bar{\boldsymbol{A}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\), and that the blocks in \(\boldsymbol{F}\) remain distinct and unmixed in \(\bar{\boldsymbol{F}}\).

A key result from applying a spatial rotation to Equation 12.3 is that \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{F}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\bar{\boldsymbol{a}}^{\rm T} \\ \bar{\boldsymbol{a}} & \bar{\boldsymbol{A}} \end{pmatrix}, \] where we see that the new 3-vector \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{a}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a} \] is just the rotated version of the original. The antisymmetric matrix transforms as \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{A}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}, \] so is the original version conjugated by rotation transformations. The next question is how to insert a second 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{b}\) into the spatial antisymmetric matrix as \(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{b})\). We can no longer straightforwardly insert a column vector and it is also not obvious how we should distribute the signs between the upper and lower triangles. However, this problem is answered uniquely by requiring that \[ \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{b})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = \boldsymbol{A}(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{b}), \tag{12.5}\] meaning the new antisymmetric matrix is one constructed by the same embedding using the rotated 3-vector \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{b}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{b}. \] So how should be embed \(\boldsymbol{b}\) into \(\boldsymbol{A}\) to ensure Equation 12.5 holds?

We use the following antisymmetric matrix basis: \[ \boldsymbol{J}_x = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & -1\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \quad \boldsymbol{J}_y = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & 0\\ -1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix},\quad \boldsymbol{J}_z = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 & 0 \\ 1 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{12.6}\] so that we can construct an antisymmetric matrix from a 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{b}\) as \[ \boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{b}) = b_x\boldsymbol{J}_x + b_y\boldsymbol{J}_y + b_z\boldsymbol{J}_z = \boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{12.7}\] where \(\boldsymbol{J} = (\boldsymbol{J}_x,\boldsymbol{J}_y,\boldsymbol{J}_z)^{\rm T}\) is the vector of basis matrices. What is special about the basis matrices \(\boldsymbol{J}_x,\boldsymbol{J}_y,\boldsymbol{J}_z\) is that they are infinitesmal generators of the Lie group SO(3) for 3D rotations mentioned earlier in Introducing 4-vectors. One consequence of the construction in Equation 12.7 is that if we apply the antisymmetric matrix \(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\) to another 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{c}\) then we get \[ (\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{c} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} c_x\\ c_y\\ c_z\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} b_yc_z - b_zc_y \\ b_zc_x - b_xc_z \\ b_xc_y - b_yc_x \end{pmatrix} \] hence we have that \[ (\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{c} = \boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}, \tag{12.8}\] so the sign structure of \(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\) encodes the 3-vector cross product. We have seen this already in Example Class 3 and we will exploit it again shortly. The key point in using the generators of rotation in Equation 12.6 to define an embedding \(\boldsymbol{A}(\boldsymbol{b})\) is that they obey \[ \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}, \] and hence guarantee that Equation 12.5 holds. You are invited to prove this a variety of different ways.

2. Rotation of generators

  1. Prove Equation 12.5 using the geometrical result that if \(\mathbf{R}_\boldsymbol{n}(\theta)\) is a rotation by \(\theta\) about an axis \(\boldsymbol{n}\) then \(\mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}_\boldsymbol{n}(\theta)\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\) is a rotation by \(\theta\) about an axis \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n}\).

  2. Prove Equation 12.5 using the rotational invariance of the 3-vector cross product \(\mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{u}\times\boldsymbol{v}) = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{u})\times(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{v})\).

  3. Prove Equation 12.5 by assuming the generators under conjugation by a rotation behave as \[ \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{J}_i\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = \sum_j R_{ji}\boldsymbol{J}_j, \tag{12.9}\] so they behave exactly the same way as basis 3-vectors would (i.e. oppositely to components).

Solution

2. Rotation of generators

  1. We can expand a rotation by \(\theta\) about an axis \(\boldsymbol{n}\) as \[ \mathbf{R}_\boldsymbol{n}(\theta) = e^{-\theta\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}}, \] where \(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}= n_x\boldsymbol{J}_x + n_y\boldsymbol{J}_y + n_z\boldsymbol{J}_z\). The geometrical fact described in the question can be written as \[ \mathbf{R}\mathbf{R}_\boldsymbol{n}(\theta)\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = \mathbf{R}_{\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n}}(\theta). \] Some thought can convince you this must be true, but a good test is to see how either side acts on \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n}\). The righthand side does nothing since it is parallel to the rotation axis, the lefthand side first rotated \(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n} \mapsto \boldsymbol{n}\), then does nothing because it is now parallel to the rotation axis, and then rotates it back to \(\boldsymbol{n} \mapsto \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n}\). The upshot is that \[ \mathbf{R}e^{-\theta\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}}\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = e^{-\theta(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}}, \] so if we differentiate with respect to \(\theta\) and evaluate at \(\theta = 0\), which extracts the generators of the rotations, then \[ \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{n}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{n})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}, \] as required to prove Equation 12.5 applies.

  2. For any 3-vectors \(\boldsymbol{u}\) and \(\boldsymbol{v}\) we have that \[ \boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{u} \times \boldsymbol{v}, \] then we also have \[ \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{v} = \mathbf{R}\big(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}(\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{v})\big) = \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{u} \times (\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{v})) = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{u})\times \boldsymbol{v}, \] after using the rotational invariance. Hence \[ \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{v} = ((\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{v}, \] and since this applies for every \(\boldsymbol{v}\) then we have \[ \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} = (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{u})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}, \] proving Equation 12.5 is true for this embedding.

  3. We have that \[ \begin{align} \mathbf{R}(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\mathbf{R}^{\rm T} &= \sum_i b_i \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{J}_i\mathbf{R}^{\rm T}, \\ &= \sum_i b_i \sum_j R_{ji}\boldsymbol{J}_j, \\ &= \sum_j \left(\sum_i R_{ji} b_i\right)\boldsymbol{J}_j, \\ &= (\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{b})\cdot\boldsymbol{J}, \end{align} \] again showing that Equation 12.5 is a specific consequence of how the generators behave under conjugation by rotation.

So we have arrived at the following matrix representation of an antisymmetric rank-2 tensor \(F^{\mu\nu}\) in terms of components of two 3-vectors \(\boldsymbol{a}\) and \(\boldsymbol{b}\) as \[ \boldsymbol{F} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -a_x & -a_y & -a_z \\ a_x & 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ a_y & b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ a_z & -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{12.10}\] which under Lorentz transformation to another frame via only a spatial rotation \(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_\mathbf{R}\) gives a new representation \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{F}} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\bar{a}_x & -\bar{a}_y & -\bar{a}_z \\ \bar{a}_x & 0 & -\bar{b}_z & \bar{b}_y \\ \bar{a}_y & \bar{b}_z & 0 & -\bar{b}_x \\ \bar{a}_z & -\bar{b}_y & \bar{b}_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \tag{12.11}\] where the new 3-vectors are the rotated versions of the original 3-vectors as \(\bar{\boldsymbol{a}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{a}\) and \(\bar{\boldsymbol{b}} = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{b}\).

Note that \(\boldsymbol{F}\) has exactly one \(x\), \(y\) and \(z\) component of either 3-vector along each row and column.

Although we have now uniquely specified how two 3-vectors \(\boldsymbol{a}\) and \(\boldsymbol{b}\) can be embedded into rank-2 antisymmetric tensor we still need to determine which corresponds to the electric \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and magnetic \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields. To figure this out we need to examine more closely the Lorentz force.

12.2 Relativistic version of the Lorentz force

The only part of the theory of electromagnetism reviewed in the last lecture which was not relativistic was the Lorentz force law for a particle with charge \(q\) \[ \boldsymbol{f}_{\rm nr} = \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{p}_{\rm nr} = q(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) , \tag{12.12}\] where \(\boldsymbol{f}_{\rm nr}\) is the non-relativistic 3-force and \(\boldsymbol{p}_{\rm nr} = m\boldsymbol{v}\) is the non-relativistic 3-momentum. However, we can readily generalise this to relativistic kinematics using the results from Example Class 3 as \[ f^\mu = c\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}p^\mu = qc F^{\mu\nu}u_\nu, \tag{12.13}\] where \(f^\mu\) is the 4-force, \(p^\mu\) is the 4-momentum and \(u^\nu\) is the 4-velocity of the particle. We also showed that the 4-force can be decomposed as \[ f^\mu = \gamma\begin{pmatrix} g \\ \boldsymbol{f} \end{pmatrix} \] where \(\boldsymbol{f}\) is the relativistic 3-force \[ \boldsymbol{f} = \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}\boldsymbol{p}, \] and that Minkowski’s equation \(f^\mu u_\mu = 0\) implies that \(cg = \boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\) is a rate of work done. Using our form of \(\boldsymbol{F}\) and \(u_\nu = (1, -\boldsymbol{v}/c)^{\rm T}\) then Equation 12.13 becomes \[ \gamma\begin{pmatrix} g \\ \boldsymbol{f} \end{pmatrix} = qc \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T} \\ \boldsymbol{a} & \boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J} \end{pmatrix} \gamma\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ -\boldsymbol{v}/c \end{pmatrix}. \] Hence we identify that \(g = qc\boldsymbol{a}^{\rm T}\boldsymbol{v} = qc\boldsymbol{a}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\) and that \[ \boldsymbol{f} = qc\boldsymbol{a} - q(\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J})\boldsymbol{v} = qc\boldsymbol{a} + q\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{b}, \tag{12.14}\] after using Equation 12.8. If we take the non-relativistic limit then \(\gamma \rightarrow 1\) and so \(\boldsymbol{p} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{p}_{\rm nr}\) as well as \(\boldsymbol{f} \rightarrow \boldsymbol{f}_{\rm nr}\). For Equation 12.14 to match Equation 12.12 we therefore need \[ \boldsymbol{a} = \boldsymbol{E}/c, \quad {\rm and} \quad \boldsymbol{b} = \boldsymbol{B}. \] We have therefore arrived at the \(({2 \atop 0})\) Faraday tensor: \[ \begin{align} F^{\mu\nu} &= [\boldsymbol{F}]_{\mu\nu},\\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -\boldsymbol{E}^{\rm T}/c \\ \boldsymbol{E}/c & \boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{J} \end{pmatrix}, \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -E_x/c & -E_y/c & -E_z/c \\ E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \tag{12.15}\] We will also encounter two other forms of the Faraday tensor. Its mixed form obtained by lowering the second index \[ {F^\mu}_\nu =\eta_{\nu\sigma}F^{\mu\sigma}, \] which we can rewrite in matrix form as \[ {F^\mu}_\nu = \eta_{\nu\sigma}F^{\mu\sigma} = [\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\eta}]_{\mu\nu}, \] where \(\boldsymbol{\eta} = \boldsymbol{\eta}^{\rm T} = {\rm diag}(1,-1,-1,-1)\) giving \[ \begin{align} {F^\mu}_\nu &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \boldsymbol{E}^{\rm T}/c \\ \boldsymbol{E}/c & -\boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{J} \end{pmatrix}, \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & E_x/c & E_y/c & E_z/c \\ E_x/c & 0 & B_z & -B_y \\ E_y/c & -B_z & 0 & B_x \\ E_z/c & B_y & -B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \tag{12.16}\] And its lower form \[ F_{\mu\nu} = \eta_{\mu\tau}\eta_{\nu\sigma}F^{\tau\sigma} = [\boldsymbol{\eta}\boldsymbol{F}\boldsymbol{\eta}]_{\mu\nu}, \] giving \[ \begin{align} F_{\mu\nu} &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \boldsymbol{E}^{\rm T}/c \\ -\boldsymbol{E}/c & \boldsymbol{B}\cdot\boldsymbol{J} \end{pmatrix}, \\ &= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & E_x/c & E_y/c & E_z/c \\ -E_x/c & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ -E_y/c & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ -E_z/c & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \tag{12.17}\]

We have to use subscript “nr” here for non-relativistic since in the lecture on Relativistic kinematics we reserved \(\boldsymbol{p} = \gamma m\boldsymbol{v}\) for the relativistic 3-momentum, and in Example Class 3 we used \(\boldsymbol{f}\) for the 3-vector component of the relativistic 4-force.
Quick cheat
  • Left multiplication by \(\boldsymbol{\eta} \cdot\) negates all spatial rows of a matrix.
  • Right multiplication by \(\cdot \boldsymbol{\eta}\) negates all spatial columns of a matrix.

A consequence of embedding the electric and magnetic fields into a rank-2 tensor is that it tells us how the field change under general Lorentz transformation between inertial frames \[ F^{{\bar \mu}{\bar \nu}} = \Lambda^{\bar \mu}_\sigma \Lambda^{\bar \nu}_\tau F^{\sigma\tau}. \tag{12.18}\] While we argued earlier that \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) will never mix due to just a spatial rotation, they will in general mix under a Lorentz boost. We know that a stationary charge will only have an electric field but a moving charge generates a current which in turn creates a magnetic field. Boosting to an arbitrary frame by \(\boldsymbol{v}\) gives fields \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}} = \gamma(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) - \frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}), \tag{12.19}\] and \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{B}} = \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2} \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right) - \frac{\gamma^2}{\gamma+1}\frac{\boldsymbol{v}}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}). \tag{12.20}\] We get a slightly less cryptic looking result if we decompose the fields as \(\boldsymbol{E} = \boldsymbol{E}_\parallel + \boldsymbol{E}_\perp\) where \(\boldsymbol{E}_\parallel\) is the field parallel with the boost \(\boldsymbol{v}\) and \(\boldsymbol{E}_\perp\) being the orthogonal part. Similarly \(\boldsymbol{B} = \boldsymbol{B}_\parallel + \boldsymbol{B}_\perp\) for the magnetic field. Then we get that \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}_\parallel = \boldsymbol{E}_\parallel, \quad {\rm and} \quad \bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_\parallel = \boldsymbol{B}_\parallel, \] and \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}_\perp = \gamma(\boldsymbol{E}_\perp + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}), \quad {\rm and} \quad \bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_\perp = \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{B}_\perp - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right). \] You might notice here that the transformations of the fields only have one factor of \(\gamma\), which defies our intuition that elements of a rank-2 object like \(F^{\mu\nu}\) should transform with \(\gamma^2\) factors. It turns out that the antisymmetric structure of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) causes cancellation of a \(\gamma\), hinting that \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is a simpler object in disguise. We will explore this idea more in the next lecture. You can see the cancellation in action by computing the transformation of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) to a new frame using Equation 12.18. You will recover the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) field transformations above (for a special case), as you are invited to do now.

Solution

3. Transforming the fields

We want to explicitly compute \[ F^{{\bar \mu}{\bar \nu}} = \Lambda^{\bar \mu}_\sigma \Lambda^{\bar \nu}_\tau F^{\sigma\tau}, \] using the Faraday tensor elements from Equation 12.15 and Lorentz transformation elements for a boost along the \(x\)-axis \[ \Lambda^{\bar{0}}_0 = \gamma, ~\Lambda^{\bar{0}}_1 = -\beta\gamma, ~\Lambda^{\bar{1}}_0 = -\beta\gamma, ~\Lambda^{\bar{1}}_1 = \gamma, ~\Lambda^{\bar{2}}_2 = 1, ~\Lambda^{\bar{3}}_3 = 1. \] We deal with the electric field parts first, namely \(F^{i0} = E_i/c\). Start with \[ F^{{\bar 1}\bar{0}} = \Lambda^{\bar 1}_\sigma \Lambda^{\bar 0}_\tau F^{\sigma\tau}, \] so only \(\sigma,\tau \in \{0,1\}\) and since \(F^{\sigma\tau} = 0\) if \(\sigma = \tau\) the contributions are \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 1}\bar{0}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 1}_0 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_1 F^{01} + \Lambda^{\bar 1}_1 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_0 F^{10},\\ &= (-\beta\gamma)^2(-E_x/c) + \gamma^2 (E_x/c), \\ &= \gamma^2(1-\beta^2)E_x/c,\\ \bar{E}_x/c &= E_x/c, \end{align} \] so we find that the electric field parallel to the boost is unchanged. For the other directions we have \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 2}\bar{0}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 2}_2 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_1 F^{21} + \Lambda^{\bar 2}_2 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_0 F^{20},\\ &= -\beta\gamma B_z + \gamma E_y/c, \\ \bar{E}_y/c &= \gamma(E_y/c -\beta B_z). \end{align} \] and similarly \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 3}\bar{0}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 3}_3 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_1 F^{31} + \Lambda^{\bar 3}_3 \Lambda^{\bar 0}_0 F^{30},\\ &= -\beta\gamma(-B_y) + \gamma E_z/c, \\ \bar{E}_z/c &= \gamma(E_z/c +\beta B_y). \end{align} \] So the electric field components orthogonal to the boost now get mixed with the magnetic field. The orthogonal (\(y\) and \(z\)) components of the electric field to the boost are summarised as \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}_\perp = (\gamma E_y - \gamma v_x B_z)\boldsymbol{e}_y + (\gamma E_z + v_x B_y) \boldsymbol{e}_z = \gamma(\boldsymbol{E}_\perp + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}), \] using \(\boldsymbol{v} = v_x\boldsymbol{e}_x\). We therefore have a special case of the general expression.

We now deal with the magnetic field components in the same way as \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 3}\bar{2}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 3}_3 \Lambda^{\bar 2}_2 F^{32},\\ \bar{B}_x &= B_x, \end{align} \] so the magnetic field parallel to the boost is unchanged as well. The other components are then \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 1}\bar{3}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 1}_0 \Lambda^{\bar 3}_3 F^{03} + \Lambda^{\bar 1}_1 \Lambda^{\bar 3}_3 F^{13},\\ &= -\beta\gamma(-E_z/c) + \gamma B_y, \\ \bar{B}_y &= \gamma(B_y + \beta E_z/c), \end{align} \] and finally \[ \begin{align} F^{{\bar 2}\bar{1}} &= \Lambda^{\bar 2}_2 \Lambda^{\bar 1}_0 F^{20} + \Lambda^{\bar 2}_2 \Lambda^{\bar 1}_1 F^{21},\\ &= -\beta\gamma E_y/c + \gamma B_z, \\ \bar{B}_z &= \gamma(B_z - \beta E_y/c). \end{align} \] The orthogonal (\(y\) and \(z\)) components of the magnetic field to the boost are summarised as \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{B}}_\perp = (\gamma B_y + \gamma v_x E_z/c^2)\boldsymbol{e}_y + (\gamma B_z - v_x E_y/c^2) \boldsymbol{e}_z = \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{B}_\perp - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right). \] We therefore have a special case of the general expression.

4. Magnetic fields are a relativistic effect

  1. Suppose in frame \(S\) we have \[ \boldsymbol{B} = 0, \qquad \boldsymbol{J} = 0. \] After a Lorentz boost with velocity \(-\boldsymbol{v}\) to frame \(\bar S\) (in which a previously stationary charge now moves with velocity \(\boldsymbol{v}\)), the electromagnetic fields transform as \[ \boldsymbol{\bar{E}} = \gamma \boldsymbol{E} - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}), \] and \[ \boldsymbol{\bar{B}} =\frac{\gamma}{c^2} \boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{E}. \] These are the standard field transformation laws Equation 12.19 and Equation 12.20 mentioned above.

  2. Consider the right-hand side of the relativistic Lorentz force (divided by \(q\)) with the transformed fields gives \[ \gamma(\boldsymbol{\bar{E}} + \boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{\bar{B}}) = \gamma \left[ \gamma \boldsymbol{E} - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) + \frac{\gamma}{c^2} \boldsymbol{v} \times (\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{E}) \right]. \] Use the vector identity \[ \boldsymbol{v} \times (\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{E}) = \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) - v^2 \boldsymbol{E}, \] the expression becomes \[ = \gamma \left[ \gamma \boldsymbol{E} - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) + \frac{\gamma}{c^2} \left( \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) - v^2 \boldsymbol{E} \right) \right]. \] Now collect terms: \[ = \gamma \left[ \gamma \boldsymbol{E}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) + \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) \left( \frac{\gamma}{c^2} - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \right) \right]. \] Using \[ 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} = \frac{1}{\gamma^2}, \] we obtain \[ \gamma \boldsymbol{E}\left(1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = \frac{1}{\gamma} \boldsymbol{E}. \] Hence the full expression simplifies to \[ = \boldsymbol{E} + \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}). \] Thus the transformed Lorentz force reduces to the purely electric force in the original frame, showing that magnetic fields arise as a relativistic effect of electric fields under Lorentz transformations.

12.3 Maxwell’s equations in relativistic form

Having found a way to embed the electric and magnetic fields into a covariant object \(F^{\mu\nu}\) our final task is to rewrite directly all four Maxwell equations in a manifestly relativistically covariant form. To do this we simply replace the components of \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) with the corresponding elements of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) (or \(F_{\mu\nu}\)) and see what we get.

12.3.1 The homogeneous equations

We start with equations (M1) and (M2) from the last lecture. Taking the scalar equation (M1) \[ \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{B} = 0, \] we get that \[ \partial_x B_x + \partial_y B_y + \partial_z B_z = \partial_1 F_{32} + \partial_2 F_{13} + \partial_3 F_{21} = 0, \tag{12.21}\] where we have used the \(({0 \atop 2})\)-type Faraday tensor from Equation 12.17. Next, we convert the vector equation (M2) as: \[ \nabla \times \boldsymbol{E} + \dot{\boldsymbol{B}} = 0, \] whose \(x\) component gives \[ \partial_y E_z - \partial_z E_y + \partial_t B_x = c(\partial_2 F_{03} + \partial_3 F_{20} + \partial_0 F_{32}) = 0, \tag{12.22}\] \(y\) component gives \[ \partial_z E_x - \partial_x E_z + \partial_t B_y = c(\partial_3 F_{01} + \partial_1 F_{30} + \partial_0 F_{13}) = 0, \tag{12.23}\] and \(z\) component gives \[ \partial_x E_y - \partial_y E_x + \partial_t B_z = c(\partial_1 F_{02} + \partial_2 F_{10} + \partial_0 F_{21}) = 0. \tag{12.24}\] Notice here if we have a \(-\) sign on a term we have chosen the element of \(F_{\mu\nu}\) which gives that sign so it no longer appears explicitly in the equation. We do this because once we drop the factor of \(c\) then Equation 12.21, Equation 12.22, Equation 12.23, and Equation 12.24 all have exactly the same structure. Namely, they contain three terms of the form \[ \partial_\mu F_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu F_{\rho\mu} + \partial_\rho F_{\mu\nu} = 0. \tag{12.25}\] The space-time indices \(\mu,\nu,\rho\) must be different since the equation vanishes identically otherwise, for example \[ \partial_3 F_{23} + \partial_2 F_{33} + \partial_3 F_{32} = \partial_3 F_{23} - \partial_3 F_{23} = 0. \] By construction whatever indices \((\mu,\nu,\rho)\) are chosen the three terms are cyclic permutations of these values. The Levi-Civita symbol

The Levi-Civita symbol \(\epsilon\) represents a collection of numbers defined from the sign of a permutation of the natural numbers \(1,2,\dots,d\) for some positive integer dimensions \(d\). Its key defining property is that it is total antisymmetry in its indices, meaning when any two indices are interchanged, equal or not, the symbol is negated \[ \epsilon_{\cdots i \cdots j \cdots} = -\epsilon_{\cdots j \cdots i \cdots}. \] An immmediate consequence of this is that the symbol is zero if any pair of indices are equal. Conventionally its value is defined as \[ \epsilon_{12\cdots d} = +1, \] and its value for any other collection of unequal indices is given by the parity of the permutation \(\mathcal{P}\) required to return it to this canonical ordering \[ \epsilon_{i_1i_2\cdots i_d} = (-1)^{\mathcal{P}}\epsilon_{12\cdots d}. \tag{12.26}\] This means that is it \(+1\) for all even permutations of \((1,2,\dots,d)\), and is negative for odd permutations. Given a \(d \times d\) matrix \(\boldsymbol{M}\) with matrix elements \(M_{ij}\) its determinant follows from the Levi-Civita symbol as \[ {\rm det}(\boldsymbol{M}) = \sum_{i_1=1}^d\sum_{i_2=1}^d\cdots\sum_{i_d=1}^d\epsilon_{i_1i_2\cdots i_d}M_{1i_1}M_{1i_2}\cdots M_{1i_d}, \] and more generally \[ \sum_{i_1=1}^d\sum_{i_2=1}^d\cdots\sum_{i_d=1}^d\epsilon_{i_1i_2\cdots i_d}M_{i_1j_1}M_{i_2j_2}\cdots M_{i_dj_d} = {\rm det}(\boldsymbol{M})\epsilon_{j_1j_2\cdots j_d}. \tag{12.27}\]

The symbol often appears in vector calculus for \(d=3\) where even permutations are given by cyclic permutation \[ \epsilon_{ijk} = \left\{\begin{matrix} +1 & (i,j,k) = (1,2,3), (2,3,1), (3,1,2) \\ -1 & (i,j,k) = (1,3,2), (3,2,1), (2,1,3) \end{matrix} \right. . \] The vector cross-product \(\boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c}\) can then be expressed (in cartesian coordinates) as \[ (\boldsymbol{b} \times \boldsymbol{c})_i = \epsilon_{ijk}b^jc^k, \] where in Euclidean space the use of upper and lower indices is only to abide by Einstein summation convention and has no further significance since the metric is simply \(\delta^{ij}\). For this reason \(\epsilon^{ijk}\) has the same elements as \(\epsilon_{ijk}\). Notice that if we just contract \(\boldsymbol{b}\) with the \(\epsilon_{ijk}\) then we get the \(3\times 3\) antisymmetric matrix from earlier \[ \epsilon_{ijk}b^j = [\boldsymbol{b}\cdot\boldsymbol{J}]_{ik}, \] showing that its sign structure is derived from the Levi-Civita symbol.

In special relativity we use the four dimensional symbol \(\epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\) with the minor modification that indices now run from \(\mu = 0,1,2,3\), and the 24 permutations split as: \[ \epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau} = \left\{\begin{matrix} +1 & (\mu,\nu,\sigma,\tau) =& (0,1,2,3), (0,2,3,1), (0,3,1,2), (2,1,3,0) \\ & & (3,1,0,2), (1,3,2,0), (3,0,2,1), (1,2,0,3) \\ & & (2,0,1,3), (1,0,3,2), (2,3,0,1), (3,2,1,0) \\ & & \\ -1 & (\mu,\nu,\sigma,\tau) =& (1,0,2,3), (1,2,3,0), (1,3,0,2), (2,0,3,1) \\ & & (3,0,1,2), (0,3,2,1), (3,2,0,1), (0,2,1,3) \\ & & (2,1,0,3), (0,1,3,2), (2,3,1,0), (3,2,0,1) \\ \end{matrix} \right. . \] Notice that cyclic permutations of \((0,1,2,3)\) no longer give other even permutation since we need \(d-1=3\) swaps. Now suppose we Lorentz transformed the \(\epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\) we get \[ \tilde{\epsilon}_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\tau}} = \Lambda^\alpha_\bar{\mu}\Lambda^\beta_\bar{\nu}\Lambda^\gamma_\bar{\sigma}\Lambda^\delta_\bar{\tau} \epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}. \] We haven’t called the transformed tensor \(\epsilon\) since we reserve this for a symbol which behaves as described in Equation 12.26. We therefore have to establish if \(\tilde{\epsilon}\) is just symbol expressed in the indices of frame \(\bar S\). Using Equation 12.27 we that the righthand side reduces to \[ \tilde{\epsilon}_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\tau}} = {\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda})\epsilon_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\tau}}. \] This means if we have a proper Lorentz transformation with \({\rm det}(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}) = 1\), e.g. boosts and rotations, then the tensor \(\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\tau}} = \epsilon_{\bar{\mu}\bar{\nu}\bar{\sigma}\bar{\tau}}\), so it has identical elements in in frame \(\bar S\) as it did in frame \(S\). This is the reason we call it a symbol. If the Lorentz transformation involves time-reversal or a spatial reflection then we get a sign flip. For this reason \(\epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\) is said to be a pseudo-tensor.

We will also have need of \(\epsilon_{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\) with all its indices raised \[ \epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau} = \eta^{\mu\alpha}\eta^{\nu\beta}\eta^{\sigma\gamma}\eta^{\tau\delta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}. \] Irrespective of our metric signature this will flip the sign, for example \[ \epsilon^{0123} = \eta^{00}\eta^{11}\eta^{22}\eta^{33}\epsilon_{0123} = -\epsilon_{0123}. \]

Due to this structure we can express all four equations (and both distinct cyclic permuations generating it) as one equation using the 4-dimensional Levi-Civita symbol \[ \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}\partial_\nu F_{\sigma\rho} = 0, \tag{12.28}\] where we generate each equation by fixing the free index \(\mu=0,1,2,3\). This is an alternative form of the Bianchi identity.

Dual Faraday tensor

Given the form of Equation 12.28 it makes sense to define a dual Faraday tensor \[ G^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}F_{\sigma\rho}. \] whose elements can be evaluated for example as \[ G^{01} = \frac{1}{2}(\epsilon^{0123}F_{23} + \epsilon^{0132}F_{32}) = \frac{1}{2}(+1)\cdot (-B_x) + \frac{1}{2}(-1)\cdot (B_x) = -B_x, \] giving in total \[ G^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -B_x & -B_y & -B_z \\ B_x & 0 & -E_z/c & E_y/c \\ B_y & E_z/c & 0 & -E_x/c \\ B_z & -E_y/c & E_x/c & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \tag{12.29}\] The dual Faraday tensor swaps the roles of the electric and magnetic fields. In this form the Bianchi identity reduces to 4-divergence \[ \partial_\nu G^{\mu\nu} = 0. \tag{12.30}\] Formally, this looks like a conservation equation for \(\mu = 0,1,2,3\) 4-currents. However, the continuity equations in this case express (M1), no magnetic monopoles, and (M2), Faraday’s law of induction. Equation 12.30 is a geometrical identity and holds independent of any dynamics or sources for the fields. It is not an equation of motion, but instead is a kinematic constraint on the theory.

The Bianchi identity has a geometrical origin and does not describe dynamics of the field, since it is independent on charges, current and the action, rather it describes what configurations of the field are physically allowed in the theory. It is a kinematic constraint defining the arena in which dynamics can occur. A simple analogy in classical mechanics would be \[ v = \frac{{\rm d}x}{{\rm d}t}, \quad {\rm and} \quad m\frac{{\rm d}v}{{\rm d}t} = F, \] the first is a kinematic constraint, defining velocity and is always true, while the second is a dynamical equation that depends on the physics, Newton’s 2nd law.

The nature of the Bianchi identity is best revealed by comparing it to the curl of a 3-vector field \(\boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{r})\). This can be written as \[ \nabla \times \boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{r})^i = \epsilon^{ijk}\partial_j w_k(\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial_y w_z(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_z w_y(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_z w_x(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_x w_z(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_x w_y(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_y w_x(\boldsymbol{r}) \end{pmatrix}, \] which is similar to Equation 12.28. Here we will justify interpretting the Bianchi identity as a “curl” of a rank-2 tensor field.

First, recall that given a scalar field \(\phi(\boldsymbol{r})\) in 3D Euclidean space we can generate a 3-vector field from this via the grad operator as \[ \nabla \phi(\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial_x\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_y\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_z\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \end{pmatrix}. \] Now taking the curl of this 3-vector field we get \[ \nabla \times \nabla \phi(\boldsymbol{r}) = \begin{pmatrix} \partial_y \partial_z\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_z \partial_y\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_z \partial_x\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_x \partial_z\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \\ \partial_x \partial_y\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) - \partial_y \partial_x\phi(\boldsymbol{r}) \end{pmatrix} = 0, \tag{12.31}\] due to the commutativity of the cartesian derivatives. The curl essentially detects that our 3-vector field is really a scalar field in disguise generated by the grad operation.

Next, consider a scalar field \(\varphi(x)\) in 1+3D space-time. We can generate a one-form field by acting with the derivative one-form operator as \(\partial_\mu\varphi(x)\). Analogous to 3-vectors we can define the “curl” of a one-form field \(V_\mu(x)\) as \[ {\rm curl}[\tilde{V}(x)]_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu V_\nu(x) - \partial_\nu V_\mu(x) \] As with Equation 12.31 we have that \[ {\rm curl}[\tilde{\partial}\varphi(x)]_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu \partial_\nu\varphi(x) - \partial_\nu \partial_\mu\varphi(x) = 0, \] again due to the commutativity of derivatives. So the one-form curl has detected that our one-form field is really a scalar field in disguise.

Note that the 3-vector curl returns another 3-vector field. The relativistic version is more elegant. The derivative one-form \(\partial_\mu\) elevates a scalar field to a one-form field (like \(\nabla\) elevates a scalar field to a 3-vector field), while the one-form curl elevates a one-form field to a rank-2 tensor field. So we can now ask what is the “curl” of a rank-2 tensor field? The answer is \[ {\rm curl}[\pmb{\symbb{T}}(x)]_{\mu\nu\rho} = \partial_\mu T_{\nu\rho}(x) + \partial_\nu T_{\rho\mu}(x) + \partial_\rho T_{\mu\nu}(x), \] which generates a rank-3 tensor field. What makes this a curl is that \[ {\rm curl}[{\rm curl}[\tilde{V}(x)]]_{\mu\nu\rho} = 0, \tag{12.32}\] once again due to the commutativity of derivatives, so the curl of a curl vanishes.

The rank-2 tensor curl therefore detects whether the input field is genuinely a rank-2 tensor field or if it is a one-form field in disguise. The key point we notice here is that the Bianchi identity is \[ {\rm curl}[\pmb{\symbb{F}}(x)]_{\mu\nu\rho} = 0, \] where \(\pmb{\symbb{F}}\) is the abstract Faraday tensor. This geometrical constraint tells us that \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is not really a rank-2 tensor, it must be the curl of a one-form field in disguise. We will explore this profound relevation in detail in the next lecture.

Expanding gives terms of the form \[ b = -\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(-\frac{E_x}{c}\right)(-B_x) + \left(-\frac{E_y}{c}\right)(-B_y) + \left(-\frac{E_z}{c}\right)(-B_z) + \dots \right]. \] Collecting all contributions (each appears four times), gives \[ b = -\frac{2}{c}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}. \]

  2. Repeating Equation 12.19 and Equation 12.20, under a boost with velocity \(\boldsymbol{v}\) the fields transform as \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}} = \gamma(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{B}) - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}), \] and \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{B}} = \gamma\left(\boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{E}\right) - \frac{\gamma^2}{c^2(\gamma+1)}\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}). \] We begin by computing \[ \begin{align} \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{E}} &= \gamma^2(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})\cdot(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) \\ & \quad - \frac{2\gamma^3}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \boldsymbol{v}\cdot(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}) \\ & \quad + \frac{\gamma^4}{c^4(\gamma+1)^2} v^2 (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2. \end{align} \] Now expand the first term: \[ (\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})^2 = |\boldsymbol{E}|^2 + 2\,\boldsymbol{E}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) + |\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}|^2. \] Using \[ |\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}|^2 = v^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 - (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})^2, \] to obtain \[ \begin{align} \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{E}} &= \gamma^2\left(|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 + 2\,\boldsymbol{E}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) + v^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 - (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})^2\right) \\ &\quad + \underbrace{\frac{2\gamma^3}{c^2(\gamma+1)} (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2 - \frac{\gamma^4}{c^4(\gamma+1)^2}v^2 (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2}_{-\frac{\gamma^2}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2}. \end{align} \] After rearrangement we obtain \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{E}} = \gamma^2\left(|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - 2\,\boldsymbol{E}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) -\frac{1}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2 + v^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 - (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})^2\right). \] Next we compute \(\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\) as: \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{B}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{B}} = \gamma^2\left(|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 - \frac{2}{c^2}\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot(\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}) - \frac{1}{c^4}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2 - \frac{1}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})^2 + \frac{v^2}{c^4}|\boldsymbol{E}|^2\right). \] Form \(|\bar{\boldsymbol{E}}|^2 - c^2|\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}|^2\) we find \[ \begin{align} |\bar{\boldsymbol{E}}|^2 - c^2|\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}|^2 &= \gamma^2\left(|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - c^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 + v^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 \right) \\ & \quad + \gamma^2\left(2\,\boldsymbol{E}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) + \frac{2}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\cdot(\boldsymbol{E}\times\boldsymbol{B}) \right) \\ & \quad - \gamma^2\left((\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})^2 - \frac{1}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})^2 \right). \end{align} \] After cancelling all the velocity-dependent terms we obtain \[ |\bar{\boldsymbol{E}}|^2 - c^2|\bar{\boldsymbol{B}}|^2 = |\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - c^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2, \] as required. The next step is to show the invariance of \(\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}\). Now compute \[ \begin{align} \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{B}} &= \gamma^2(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) \cdot \left(\boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right)\\ &\quad + \frac{\gamma^4}{c^4(\gamma+1)^2} v^2 (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})\\ &\quad - \frac{\gamma^3}{c^2(\gamma+1)} \left[(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B})\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot(\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})+(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})\,\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\left(\boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}\right)\right]. \end{align} \] Expand the first term: \[ \begin{align} (\boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) \cdot (\boldsymbol{B} - \frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}) &= \boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B} + \boldsymbol{B}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}) \\ & \quad -\frac{1}{c^2}\boldsymbol{E}\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}) - \frac{1}{c^2}(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}). \end{align} \] and using \[ (\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B})\cdot(\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{E}) = v^2\boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B} - (\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{E})(\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}), \] we find the complete cancellation of velocity-dependent contributions. Thus \[ \bar{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot\bar{\boldsymbol{B}} = \boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}, \] meaning that both scalar quantities \[ |\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - c^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2, \qquad \boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B} \] are Lorentz invariant.

  3. For a plane wave, \[ \boldsymbol{E} = \boldsymbol{E}^{(0)} e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x} - \omega t)}, \qquad \boldsymbol{B} = \boldsymbol{B}^{(0)} e^{i(\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{x} - \omega t)}. \] From Maxwell’s equations, \[ \boldsymbol{k}\times\boldsymbol{E}^{(0)} = \omega \boldsymbol{B}^{(0)}, \] and \[ \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{E}^{(0)} = 0, \qquad \boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{B}^{(0)} = 0. \] Thus \[ \boldsymbol{E}\cdot\boldsymbol{B} = 0, \] and \[ |\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - c^2|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 = 0. \] Hence both invariants vanish for a plane electromagnetic wave: \[ a = 0, \qquad b = 0. \]

12.3.2 The inhomogeneous equations

Let’s now tackle Maxwell’s equation (M3) and (M4). These equations involve the charge density \(\rho(x)\) and 3-current density \(\boldsymbol{j}(x)\) in a given frame \(S\). We saw earlier in the lecture Plane-waves and particle flow we saw that these objects can be readily packaged into a 4-current \[ j^{\mu}(x) = \left(\begin{array}{c} \rho(x)c \\ {\boldsymbol{j}}(x)\end{array}\right) = \rho_0(x)u^\mu(x)c, \] where \(\rho_0(x)\) is the co-moving charge density and \(u^\mu(x)\) is the 4-velocity field of the particles. With this the scalar equation (M3) \[ \epsilon_0 \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{E} = \epsilon_0(\partial_x E_x + \partial_y E_y + \partial_z E_z) = \rho, \] converts using the \(({2 \atop 0})\) Faraday tensor from Equation 12.15 as \[ c\left(\partial_1 F^{10} + \partial_2 F^{20} + \partial_3 F^{30}\right) = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0} = c\mu_0 j^0, \] after using \(c^2 = (\epsilon_0\mu_0)^{-1}\). Finally, we convert vector equation (M4) as: \[ \mu_0^{-1}\nabla\times\boldsymbol{B} - \epsilon_0 \dot{\boldsymbol{E}} = \boldsymbol{j}, \] whose \(x\) component gives \[ \mu_0^{-1}(\partial_y B_z - \partial_z B_y) - \epsilon_0\partial_t E_x = j_x ~\rightarrow ~ \mu_0^{-1}(\partial_2 F^{21} + \partial_3 F^{31} + \partial_0 F^{01}) = j^1. \] using \(c^2\epsilon = \mu_0^{-1}\) and picking elements of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) to replace the fields to make all terms have the same sign. Likewise for the \(y\) we get \[ \mu_0^{-1}(\partial_z B_x - \partial_x B_z) - \epsilon_0\partial_t E_y = j_y ~ \rightarrow ~\mu_0^{-1}(\partial_3 F^{32} + \partial_1 F^{12} + \partial_0 F^{02}) = j^2. \] and the \(z\) as: \[ \mu_0^{-1}(\partial_x B_y - \partial_y B_x) - \epsilon_0\partial_t E_z = j_z ~ \rightarrow ~\mu_0^{-1}(\partial_1 F^{13} + \partial_2 F^{21} + \partial_0 F^{03}) = j^3. \] All four of these equations can be compactly encoded as \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0j^\nu. \] This is called the relativistic Maxwell equation. In contrast to the Bianchi identity this equation expresses how the dynamics of the EM fields will occur in the presence of charges and currents. It is this equation which we will aim to derive via an appropriate EM Lagrangian in the next lecture.
