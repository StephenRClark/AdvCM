13  Lagrangian approach to EM

We have shown how the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields can be embedded into the Faraday tensor \(F^{\mu\nu}\) giving a fully relativistic formulation of EM. In this lecture we will re-express this again in terms of a relativistic 4-vector potential which naturally leads to a Lagrangian formulation the the EM field.

13.1 The 4-vector potential

In the last lecture we saw several features of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) which mean that a rank-2 tensor is not the covariant object fundamentally describing electromagnetism. First, the Faraday tensor is antisymmetric which results in the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields transforming between frames with a factor of \(\gamma\) rather than \(\gamma^2\) expected for elements of a general rank-2 tensor. Second, \(F^{\mu\nu}\) obeys the Bianchi identity which is a kinematic constraint akin to fixing the “curl” of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) to be zero.

We already know an alternative description of electromagnetism. Specifically the scalar potential \(V\) and 3-vector potential \(\boldsymbol{A}\), which gives rise to the fields \[ \boldsymbol{E} = - \dot{\boldsymbol{A}} - \nabla V, \quad \boldsymbol{B} = \nabla\times\boldsymbol{A}, \tag{13.1}\] as reviewed in Revision of Electromagnetism. Once again in this course we see a situation where we have a scalar quantity and a 3-vector quantity which are intimately related. It would therefore seem very sensible to explore the idea that they in fact should be combined together into a 4-vector potential \[ A^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} V/c \\ \boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}, \quad \text{and so} \quad A_\mu = \begin{pmatrix} V/c \\ -\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{13.2}\] One way to justify that \(A^\mu\) is a genuine 4-vector is to consider the interaction of an electromagnetic field with charged matter.

Justifying \(A^\mu\) is a 4-vector

Our work in Example Class 3 in question 3. can motivate this form. There we considered the relativistic Lagrangian for a single particle interacting with a putative “4-potential” \[ \phi^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} \Phi \\ \boldsymbol{\phi} \end{pmatrix} \] through its 4-velocity \(u^\mu\) as \[ L = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} - \frac{\phi^\mu u_\mu}{\gamma} = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} - \Phi + \frac{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c}. \tag{13.3}\] While this Lagrangian is not Lorentz scalar, the action arising from it should be \[ \mathcal{S} \equiv \int_{t_0}^{t_1}{\rm d}t\, L = \int_{s_0}^{s_1}{\rm d}s\,\frac{\gamma}{c}L, \] and hence \[ \mathcal{S} = \int_{s_0}^{s_1}{\rm d}s\,\left(-mc - \frac{1}{c}\phi^\mu u_\mu\right). \] From this we see that \(\phi^\mu\) must be a genuine 4-vector if the contraction \(\phi^\mu u_\mu\), and therefore \(\mathcal{S}\) itself, is to be Lorentz scalar.

We found that the non-relativistic limit of the Lagrangian in Equation 13.3 was \[ L \approx -mc^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \Phi + \frac{\boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c}. \] Ignoring the rest mass constant and comparing this to the non-relativistic Lorentz force Lagrangian from Revision of Electromagnetism \[ L = \tfrac{1}{2}m \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} - q V + q \boldsymbol{v}\cdot\boldsymbol{A}, \] we identify that \(\phi^\mu = qc A^\mu\) or \[ \begin{pmatrix} \Phi \\ \boldsymbol{\phi} \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} qV \\ qc\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{13.4}\] Since \(c\) and \(q\) are Lorentz scalars this identification means that \(A^\mu\) must be a 4-vector.

Notice that the quantity \[ qc u^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} qc \\ q\boldsymbol{v} \end{pmatrix} \] looks like a 4-current generated by point-like charged particle (a \(\delta\)-function field in space-time) which we would expect for a general matter field \[ j^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} \rho(x)c \\ \boldsymbol{j}(x) \end{pmatrix}. \] Based on this we anticipate an interaction of a matter field with an EM field of the form \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm int} = -A^\mu j_\mu \tag{13.5}\] in the Lagrangian density. We will put this to use shortly.

Note on spatial indices and 3-vectors

For 3-vectors like \(\boldsymbol{B}\) we denote its components as \(B_i\) for \(i=1,2,3\). We can raise its index to \(B^j = \delta^{ij}B_i\) using the Euclidean metric, which means there is no difference between upper and lower indices. However, we use \(A^i\) to denote the spatial components of the 4-vector potential \(A^\mu\) in Equation 13.2. We don’t ever refer directly to the components of the 3-vector potential \(\boldsymbol{A}\), which means that lower index version \(A_j = -\boldsymbol{A}\cdot{e}_j\) since it refers to the spatial components of a one-form \(A_\mu\). Likewise \(\partial_j\) are the spatial components of the derivative one-form \(\partial_\mu\). So whenever we replace \(\nabla\) in any 3-vector expressions we use that \(\partial_j = \boldsymbol{e}_j\cdot\nabla\), but \(\partial^j = -\boldsymbol{e}_j\cdot\nabla\) since they are the spatial component of the 4-vector derivative.

It is instructive to convert Equation 13.1 into an expression involving \(F^{\mu\nu}\). Starting with the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) field where \(E_j/c = F_{0j}\) we have \[ \boldsymbol{E} = - \dot{\boldsymbol{A}} - \nabla V \quad \to \quad cF_{0j} = c\partial_0 A_j -c\partial_j A_0. \tag{13.6}\] For the \(\boldsymbol{B}\) field we have that \[ B^i = (\nabla \times \boldsymbol{A})^i = -\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_j A_k, \] but also \(F_{ij} = \epsilon_{ikj}B^k\). Hence \[ \begin{align} F_{mn} &= -\epsilon_{min}\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_j A_k,\\ &=\epsilon_{imn}\epsilon^{ijk}\partial_j A_k, \\ \end{align} \] which are using the identity \[ \epsilon_{imn}\epsilon^{ijk} = \delta^j_m\delta^k_n - \delta^k_m\delta^j_n, \tag{13.7}\] gives \[ \begin{align} F_{mn} &= \left(\delta^j_m\delta^k_n - \delta^k_m\delta^j_n\right)\partial_j A_k, \\ &= \partial_m A_n - \partial_n A_m. \end{align} \tag{13.8}\] Putting Equation 13.6 and Equation 13.8 together we have that \[ F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu. \tag{13.9}\] We have therefore shown that the Faraday tensor is the curl of the 4-vector potential. This construction is a key result since:

  • it is manifestly antisymmetric, and
  • it automatically satisfies the Bianchi identity, e.g. the curl of a curl = 0.

The latter point means that we get half of Maxwell’s equations, e.g. the homogeneous ones which correspond to kinematic constraints of the theory, without any further work just by using Equation 13.9.

Work-along exercises

1. \(\epsilon\) identity

Prove the \(\epsilon\) symbol identity given in Equation 13.7

2. Vector triple product

Show that Equation 13.7 is nothing other than the vector triple product identity \[ \boldsymbol{u} \times (\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{w}) = \boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}) - \boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}), \] for three 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{u},\boldsymbol{v},\boldsymbol{w}\).

Solution

1. \(\epsilon\) identity

In Equation 13.7 the repeated index implies the summation \[ \epsilon_{imn}\epsilon^{ijk} = \epsilon_{1mn}\epsilon^{1jk} + \epsilon_{2mn}\epsilon^{2jk} + \epsilon_{3mn}\epsilon^{3jk}. \] Recalling that \[ \epsilon_{ijk} = \epsilon^{ijk} = \left\{ \begin{array}{rl} +1 & \hbox{if $(i,j,k) = (1,2,3), (2,3,1), (3,1,2)$} \\ -1 & \hbox{if $(i,j,k) = (1,3,2), (2,1,3), (3,2,1)$} \\ 0 & \hbox{otherwise} \end{array} \right. \] we now examine what the value is for various choices of \((j,k)\) and \((m,n)\). Only a few possible indices give non-zero value owing to the \(\epsilon\) symbols with each term contributing:

\(i\) \((j,k)(m,n)\) value
\(1\) \((2,3)(2,3)\), \(\quad(3,2)(3,2)\) \(+1\)
\((2,3)(3,2)\), \(\quad(3,2)(2,3)\) \(-1\)
\(2\) \((3,1)(3,1)\), \(\quad(1,3)(1,3)\) \(+1\)
\((3,1)(1,3)\), \(\quad(1,3)(3,1)\) \(-1\)
\(3\) \((2,1)(2,1)\), \(\quad(1,2)(1,2)\) \(+1\)
\((2,1)(1,2)\), \(\quad(1,2)(2,1)\) \(-1\)

We can then compare this to Equation 13.7 where we see that the expression \(\delta^j_m\delta^k_n - \delta^k_m\delta^j_n\) gives a \(+1\) if \(m=j\) and \(n=k\), and \(-1\) for \(m=k\) and \(n=j\), exactly as is the case in the table above.

2. Vector triple product

We use that \[ (\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{w})^i = \epsilon^{ijk}v_jw_k, \] so \[ \boldsymbol{u} \times (\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{w}) = \epsilon_{mni}u^n(\boldsymbol{v} \times \boldsymbol{w})^i = \epsilon_{imn}\epsilon^{ijk}u^nv_jw_k, \] and so involves precisely the contraction of \(\epsilon\) symbols in Equation 13.7. Applying that identity we get \[ \begin{align} (\delta^j_m\delta^k_n - \delta^k_m\delta^j_n)u^nv_jw_k &= \delta^j_m v_j u^kv_k - \delta^k_m w_k u^j v_j, \\ &= v_m (u^kv_k) - w_m (u^j v_j), \\ &= (\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}) - \boldsymbol{w}(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}))_m, \end{align} \] so we recover the vector triple product.

13.2 Gauge transformation of 4-vector potential

We know that the scalar and vector potential are not unique. Indeed, as discussed in Revision of Electromagnetism, the physical \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields are invariant to gauge transformations: \[ \boldsymbol{A} \to \boldsymbol{A} + \nabla\chi, \qquad V \to V - \dot{\chi}, \tag{13.10}\] where \(\chi\) is an arbitrary but smooth differentiable function of space-time. We can readily encapsulate the gauge transformation in Equation 13.10 into the relativistic formalism as the 4-vector potential mapping as \[ A_\mu \to A_\mu - \partial_\mu \chi, \tag{13.11}\] after recognising that \[ A_\mu = \begin{pmatrix} V/c \\ -\boldsymbol{A} \end{pmatrix}, \quad {\rm and} \quad \partial_\mu\chi = \begin{pmatrix} \dot{\chi}/c \\ \nabla \chi \end{pmatrix}. \] Given its elements are the physical fields the Faraday tensor itself is gauge invariant.

For practical calculations in EM we often fix a gauge for convenience. The expression of the Lorenz gauge \[ \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{A} + \frac{1}{c^2}\dot{V} = 0, \] for the 4-vector potential is particularly appealing \[ \partial_\mu A^\mu = 0, \] since it fixes the 4-divergence of the 4-vector potential to be zero. The relativistic Maxwell equation simplifies in this gauge to \[ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu = \mu_0 j^\nu. \tag{13.12}\] So in the Lorenz gauge the 4-vector potential obeys the inhomogeneous wave-equation for each of its components. While the Lorenz gauge does constrain the 4-vector potential it does not remove all gauge freedom from it. Given \(\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0\) performing a gauge transformation gives \[ \partial_\mu A^\mu \to \partial_\mu (A^\mu - \partial^\mu \chi) \] which will still satisfy the Lorenz gauge so long as \(\partial_\mu\partial^\mu\chi = 0\). In otherwords we can add the derivative of solution of the scalar wave-equation to the 4-vector potential and remain in the Lorenz gauge.

Prove that \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is gauge invariant as claimed.

4. Lorenz gauge equation of motion

Confirm Equation 13.12 arises from the relativistic Maxwell equation in the Lorenz gauge.

Solution

3. Gauge invariance of \(F^{\mu\nu}\)

This is readily confirmed by substituting Equation 13.11 into Equation 13.9 to get \[ \begin{align} F_{\mu\nu} &\to \partial_\mu (A_\nu - \partial_\nu \chi) - \partial_\nu (A_\mu - \partial_\mu \chi), \\ & \to \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu - \partial_\mu\partial_\nu\chi + \partial_\nu\partial_\mu\chi, \\ & \to F_{\mu\nu}, \end{align} \] after using the commutativity of the derivatives.

4. Lorenz gauge equation of motion

Expand out the Faraday tensor in the relativistic Maxwell equation and then apply the Lorenz gauge condition to get: \[ \begin{align} \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} &= \mu_0 j^\nu, \\ \partial_\mu(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu) &= \mu_0 j^\nu,\\ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu(\partial_\mu A^\mu) &= \mu_0 j^\nu,\\ \partial_\mu\partial^\mu A^\nu &= \mu_0 j^\nu. \end{align} \]

13.3 EM Lagrangian

We have successfully rewritten Maxwell’s equation in a relativistically covariant form. In doing so we have also generalised the only part of conventional electromagnetism that wasn’t already relativistic, namely the Lorentz force law, into a relativistic form also. What now remains for us to do is to construct a Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) whose Euler-Lagrange equations produce the dynamical equation \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0j^\nu, \tag{13.13}\] whilst always satisfying the kinematic constraint \[ \partial_\mu F_{\nu\rho} + \partial_\nu F_{\rho\mu} + \partial_\rho F_{\mu\nu} = 0. \tag{13.14}\]

To apply the variational principle we choose some variables that specify the configuration of the field and then vary them freely. The Euler-Lagrange equations come from requiring stationarity for all admissible variations. To proceed we need to we need to figure out what are the truely independent variables for electromagnetism. Do we vary the:

  • physical \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields, so elements of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) themselves, or
  • 4-vector potential \(A^\mu\)?

At first sight one might think that the Faraday tensor and the physical fields should be the natural object to base our variations on. For example it would be perfectly reasonable to consider \[ F^{\mu\nu} \mapsto F^{\mu\nu} + \delta F^{\mu\nu}, \] where \(\delta F^{\mu\nu}\) is an arbitrary antisymmetric variation. The problem is that the six components of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) are not independent. They must obey Equation 13.14 which represents a kinematic constraint that must be obeyed if the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) inside \(F^{\mu\nu}\) are physically allowed fields. Consequently they are not independent variables. A variation such as \(\delta F^{\mu\nu}\) would not preserve a solution to the Bianchi equation and would therefore take us outside physically admissible configuration space of the theory. This resonates with what we discussed in the last lecture that the Bianchi identity tells us that \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is not really a rank-2 antisymmetric tensor, but is really the curl of a 4-vector field.

A simple analogy would be to describe variations of a particle’s location constrained to be on the surface of a sphere. If we used arbitrary variations in its cartesian coordinates \(\delta x, \delta y, \delta z\) then we would certainly move off the surface requiring a Lagrange multiplier to fix. Or we could use arbitrary variations \(\delta\theta,\delta\phi\) of angular coordinates which automatically stay on the surface.

Consequently, the varying the 4-vector potential is the most sensible approach. We use the variation \[ A^\mu \mapsto A^\mu + \delta A^\mu, \] and from this generate \(F_{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu\) as a 4-vector curl. For any such variations \(F_{\mu\nu}\) will be antisymmetric and automatically satisfies Equation 13.14. This all points to \(A^\mu\) instead being the fundamental description of electromagnetism, consistent also with it being the simplest way to locally couple to charged matter, e.g. the \(-A^\mu j_\mu\) term in Equation 13.5.

Also quantum effects like the Aharonov-Bohm effect make it clear that \(A^\mu\) is not just bookkeeping device, but rather it is a physical meaningful quantity, despite its gauge redundancy.

Recall from Building a field theory that a 4-vector field theory is derived from a relativistic Euler-Lagrange equation of the form \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial A_\nu} - \partial_\mu \left(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu}\right) = 0. \] The next question is what is \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\)? The answer to this should be a:

  • Lorentz scalar, as with all relativistic field theories, and
  • gauge invariant, so it depends on the physical \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields.

These suggest strongly that we should build \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) from \(F^{\mu\nu}\). The two most obvious Lorentz scalars we can construct from the Faraday tensor are its trace \({F^\mu}_\mu = 0\), which is not useful, and its full contraction with itself \(F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\). We therefore conjecture that in the absence of any sources (so a free field) it is governed by the Lagrangian \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}. \] We can motivate why this looks sensible. We expect wave solutions to arise the EM Lagrangian, indeed we saw already that in the Lorenz gauge the relativistic Maxwell’s equation reduces to a wave equation for \(A^\mu\) in Equation 13.12. For a scalar field the Lagrangian giving a wave-equation is \[ \mathcal{L}_{d'A} = \partial_\mu\varphi\partial^\mu\varphi, \] which gives an Euler-Lagrange equation \(\partial_\mu\partial^\mu \varphi = 0\), while for a 4-vector field it is \[ \mathcal{L}_{4d'A} = \partial_\mu a_\nu\partial^\mu a^\nu, \tag{13.15}\] giving \(\partial_\mu\partial^\mu a_\nu = 0\). Expanding \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) in the 4-vector potential \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0}\left(\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu\right)\left(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu\right), \] we recognise that it contains terms like \(\partial_\mu A_\nu\partial^\mu A^\nu\) with exactly the same form as in Equation 13.15. You will also recognise, from work-along exercise 7. in the last lecture, that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) is one of the Lorentz invariants of the EM field where \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 - \frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\boldsymbol{B}|^2, \tag{13.16}\] further motivating it as a sensible choice.

Our next step is to compute the Euler-Lagrange equations for \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\). We immediately see that \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial A_\nu} = 0, \] so our main work is to compute the other derivative \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu}\left(F_{\sigma\tau} F^{\sigma\tau}\right), \] which is found to be \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\nu}. \tag{13.17}\]

Work-along exercises

5. Derivative of EM Lagrangian

Complete the calculation of \(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu}\) to confirm the result given in Equation 13.17.

Solution

5. Derivative of EM Lagrangian

We need to compute \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu}\left(F_{\sigma\tau} F^{\sigma\tau}\right). \] This calcularion is greatly simplified by using the product rule \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu} (F_{\sigma\tau} F^{\sigma\tau}) = \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu} F_{\sigma\tau}\right) F^{\sigma\tau} + F_{\sigma\tau}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu} F^{\sigma\tau}\right) = 2\frac{\partial F_{\sigma\tau}}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu} F^{\sigma\tau}, \]
and combining terms after “see-sawing” the indices. We are then left to compute \[ \frac{\partial F_{\sigma\tau}}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu} = \frac{\partial}{\partial \partial_{\mu} A_\nu}(\partial_\sigma A_\tau - \partial_\tau A_\sigma) = \delta^\mu_\sigma\delta^\nu_\tau - \delta^\mu_\tau\delta^\nu_\sigma. \] Note it is essential to use appropriate independent indices in this calculation to ensure contractions are not muddled up. Putting this all together we get \[ \begin{align} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} &= -\frac{1}{2\mu_0}(\delta^\mu_\sigma\delta^\nu_\tau - \delta^\mu_\tau\delta^\nu_\sigma)F^{\sigma\tau}, \\ &= -\frac{1}{2\mu_0}(F^{\mu\nu} - F^{\nu\mu}), \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\nu}. \end{align} \] as claimed in Equation 13.17.

The Euler-Lagrange equation is then \[ -\partial_\mu \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0, \] which is the relativistic Maxwell equation with no sources.

To deal with the inhomogeneous case we add the interaction term with a matter field electric 4-current \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM-int} = \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM} + \mathcal{L}_{\rm int} = -\frac{1}{4\mu_0}F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu} - A_\mu j^\mu. \tag{13.18}\] The same calculation now has \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM-int}}{\partial A_\nu} = -j^\nu, \] while the other term remains as \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM-int}}{\partial \partial_\mu A_\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\nu}, \] so the Euler-Lagrange equation becomes \[ -j^\nu + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = 0 \] hence we recover the relativistic Maxwell equation \[ \partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 j^\nu. \] The construction of \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM-int}\) in Equation 13.18 illustrates a very general principle in field theory. Namely, that we build Lagrangians by first determining a “free” field contribution, \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) in this case, and then add contribution to account for its interaction with another field \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}\). To complete this story we should also add a “free” field contribution for this second field as well to give a full Lagrangian for two interacting fields. In our case this would be a Lagrangian for “electrodynamics” of the form \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm ED} = \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM} + \mathcal{L}_{\rm int} + \mathcal{L}_{\rm matter}, \tag{13.19}\] where \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm matter}\) describes the dynamics of the matter which generates the 4-current \(j^\mu\). We can already see that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm matter}\) is essential since the interaction term, which depends explicitly on the 4-vector potential \(A_\mu\), is not gauge invariant, as we originally demanded \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) be. This tells us that the matter field must also change whenever a gauge transformation is made, and do so in such a way that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}\) becomes gauge invariant. Fleshing this out and building \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm matter}\) is not formally a part of this course, but we will get a taste for what it looks like in the final non-examinable lecture of this course on relativistic quantum mechanics.

13.4 The EM stress-energy tensor

Now we have arrived at an electromagnetic Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) for a free field we should apply Noether’s theorem and compute its corresponding stress-energy tensor. Back in Symmetries and Noether’s theorem we derived the stress-energy tensor for a scalar field \(\varphi(x)\) as \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial(\partial_\mu\varphi)} \partial^\nu \varphi - \mathcal{L}\eta^{\mu\nu}, \tag{13.20}\] based on translating the field in space-time by \(a^\mu\) as \[ \varphi(x) \mapsto \varphi(x+a) = \varphi(x) + \overbrace{a^\mu\partial_\mu\varphi(x)}^{\delta_S\varphi(x)} + \dots \] For a one-form field \(A_\mu(x)\) of the 4-vector potential in EM we have an analogous symmetry induced variation \[ A_\mu(x) \mapsto A_\mu(x+a) = A_\mu(x) + \overbrace{a^\nu\partial_\nu A_\mu(x)}^{\delta_S A_\mu(x)} + \dots \] so the only difference is the \(\mu\) of the field. The same derivation therefore arrives at the obvious generalisation of Equation 13.20 to a one-form field to get its “canonical” stress-energy tensor as \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}}{\partial(\partial_\mu A_\sigma)} \partial^\nu A_\sigma - \mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\eta^{\mu\nu}. \tag{13.21}\] We can evaluate this result immediately using Equation 13.17 to find \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\sigma}\partial^\nu A_\sigma + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}. \tag{13.22}\] However, there are two major problems with this result:

  1. It is not symmetric so \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} \neq T^{\nu\mu}_{\rm can}\). This causes deep issues for general relativity since the stress-energy tensor is the source of gravity and the Einstein field tensor \(G^{\mu\nu}\) it generates must be symmetric, which is not manifest if \(T^{\mu\nu}\) isn’t. Also a lack of symmetry in the stress-energy tensor points to orbital angular momentum not being locally conserved.

  2. It is not gauge invariant! The stress-energy tensor contains physical observables like the energy density and momentum fluxes of the field which must be gauge invariant. We also know from classical electromagnetism that these can be expressed in terms of the \(\boldsymbol{E}\) and \(\boldsymbol{B}\) fields which are gauge invariant.

Both these issues can be solved in a process called Belifante-Rosenfeld symmetrisation. We will only sketch the details here. The idea is to modify the stress-energy tensor as \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} = T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} + \partial_\rho \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu}, \] where \(\Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu}\) is called the Belifante tensor which is antisymmetric in its first two indices \[ \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu} = -\Gamma^{\mu\rho\nu}. \] We are free to add such a term to the stress-energy tensor since it does not effect its 4-divergence \[ \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} + \partial_\mu\partial_\rho \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} = 0, \] where we used the symmetry of the derivatives \(\partial_\mu\partial_\rho\) and antisymmetry of \(\Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu}\). For this trick to be useful we need to be able to readily construct a Belifante tensor using the basic ingredients of our theory. A good choice is \[ \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\rho\mu}A^\nu, \tag{13.23}\] which has the required property and whose form is motivated by its similarity to the first term Equation 13.22. Adding the 4-divergence of this term fixes the EM stress-energy as \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\eta^{\nu\alpha}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\mu\beta} + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}. \tag{13.24}\] Since this new version depends only on the Faraday tensor it is gauge invariant. It is easy to confirm that it is also symmetric.

Check that \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}\) in Equation 13.24 is symmetric as claimed.

Solution

6. Belifante-Rosenfeld symmetrisation

Given \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\sigma}\partial^\nu A_\sigma + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}, \] we can focus our attention on the first term, since the second is already symmetric and gauge invariant. First using Equation 13.23 we compute \[ \partial_\rho \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\partial_\rho (F^{\rho\mu}A^\nu) = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\left(A^\nu \partial_\rho F^{\rho\mu} + F^{\rho\mu}\partial_\rho A^\nu\right) = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\rho\mu}\partial_\rho A^\nu, \] where we have used that \(\partial_\rho F^{\rho\mu} = 0\) for a free EM field. Hence \[ \begin{align} T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} &= T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm can} + \partial_\rho \Gamma^{\rho\mu\nu}, \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\sigma}\partial^\nu A_\sigma + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta} -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\rho\mu}\partial_\rho A^\nu, \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\left(F^{\mu\sigma}\partial^\nu A_\sigma + F^{\sigma\mu}\partial_\sigma A^\nu\right) + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}, \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\sigma}\left(\partial^\nu A_\sigma - \partial_\sigma A^\nu\right) + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}, \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}F^{\mu\sigma}{F^\nu}_\sigma + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}, \\ &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\eta^{\nu\alpha}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\mu\beta} + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\nu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}, \end{align} \] where the antisymmetry of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) has been used along with several dummy index relabellings along the way. We therefore recover Equation 13.24 as required.

7. Symmetric stress-energy tensor

Using Equation 13.24 we have the swapped version \[ T^{\nu\mu}_{\rm EM} = -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\eta^{\mu\alpha}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\nu\beta} + \frac{1}{4\mu_0}\eta^{\nu\mu}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\alpha\beta}. \] The second term is symmetric since the metric is \(\eta^{\nu\mu}=\eta^{\mu\nu}\). For the first term the following index manipulations with the metric give \[ \eta^{\mu\alpha}F_{\alpha\sigma}F^{\nu\sigma} = \eta_{\sigma\beta}F^{\mu\beta}F^{\nu\sigma} = \eta^{\nu\alpha}F_{\alpha\beta}F^{\mu\beta}, \] showing it is also symmetric, hence \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} = T^{\nu\mu}_{\rm EM}\).

Armed with \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}\) it makes sense to evaluate the 00 element directly as \[ \begin{align} T^{00}_{\rm EM} &= -\frac{1}{\mu_0}\eta^{00}F_{0i}F^{0i} - \eta^{00}\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}, \\ &= \frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{|\boldsymbol{E}|^2}{c^2} - \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 + \frac{\mu_0}{2}|\boldsymbol{B}|^2, \\ &= \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}|\boldsymbol{E}|^2 + \frac{1}{2\mu_0}|\boldsymbol{B}|^2 = W, \end{align} \] where we have used Equation 13.16 to express \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) in terms of the physical fields. What we recover is that \(T^{00}_{\rm EM}\) is the energy density of the EM field \(W\), as reviewed in Revision of Electromagnetism. Examining all the elements of \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}\) reveals it has the structure \[ T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} = \begin{pmatrix} W & \boldsymbol{S}^{\rm T}/c \\ \boldsymbol{S}/c & -\boldsymbol{\sigma} \end{pmatrix}, \tag{13.25}\] where \(\boldsymbol{S} =\mu_0^{-1} \boldsymbol{E} \times \boldsymbol{B}\) is the Poynting vector and \[ [\boldsymbol{\sigma}]_{ij} = \epsilon_0 E_iE_j + \mu_0^{-1}B_iB_j - W\delta_{ij}, \] is the Maxwell stress tensor. As required \(T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM}\) encompasses the dynamics of energy and momentum in the EM field.

  2. The 4-vector \(f^{\mu} \equiv F^{\mu\tau}j_{\tau}\) is called the Lorentz force density. It is the natural generalization of the relativistic Lorentz force to a continuous charged field with 4-current \(j^\nu\). Show that the following expression relating it to the electromagnetic stress-energy tensor, \[ \partial_{\nu} T^{\mu\nu} = -f^{\mu}, \] can be rewritten as \[ \begin{eqnarray} \dot{W} + \nabla \cdot \boldsymbol{S} & = & -\boldsymbol{j}\cdot\boldsymbol{E} \\ c^{-2} \dot{\boldsymbol{S}} - \nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma} & = & - \rho \boldsymbol{E} - \boldsymbol{j} \times \boldsymbol{B}, \end{eqnarray} \] where \((\nabla\cdot\boldsymbol{\sigma})^k = \partial_j \sigma^{jk}\).

Solution

8. Electromagnetic stress-energy tensor

  1. Take the stress-energy tensor \[ \begin{align} T^{\mu\nu}_{\rm EM} &= \mu_0^{-1} \left(-F^{\mu\rho} F^\nu_{\ \rho} + \frac{1}{4} \eta^{\mu\nu} F^{\rho\sigma}F_{\rho\sigma}\right), \\ &= \mu_0^{-1} \left( - F^{\mu\rho} F^\nu_{\ \rho} - \frac{1}{2} \eta^{\mu\nu} a \right), \end{align} \] where \(a = \frac{E^2}{c^2} - B^2\) is the EM scalar invariant found before. Now \[ - F^{\mu\rho} F^\nu_{\ \rho} = F^\nu_{\ \rho} F^{\rho\mu} \] can be expressed as a conventional matrix product involving the matrices \[ F^\nu_{\ \rho} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & E_x & E_y & E_z \\ E_x & 0 & B_z & -B_y \\ E_y & -B_z & 0 & B_x \\ E_z & B_y & -B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \] \[ F^{\rho\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -E_x & -E_y & -E_z \\ E_x & 0 & -B_z & B_y \\ E_y & B_z & 0 & -B_x \\ E_z & -B_y & B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}. \] For simplicity we have set \(c = 1\), and will restore \(c^{-1}\) on the electric field component at the end. Thus \[ F^\nu_{\ \rho} F^{\rho\mu} = \begin{pmatrix} E^2 & B_z E_y - B_y E_z & B_x E_z - B_z E_x & B_y E_x - B_x E_y \\ B_z E_y - B_y E_z & -E_x^2 + B_z^2 + B_y^2 & -E_x E_y - B_x B_y & -E_x E_z - B_x B_z \\ B_x E_z - B_z E_x & -E_x E_y - B_x B_y & -E_y^2 + B_z^2 + B_x^2 & -E_y E_z - B_y B_z \\ B_y E_x - B_x E_y & -E_x E_z - B_x B_z & -E_y E_z - B_y B_z & -E_z^2 + B_y^2 + B_x^2 \end{pmatrix}, \] and \[ \frac{1}{2} \eta^{\mu\nu} a = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{B^2 - E^2}{2} & 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{E^2 - B^2}{2} & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & \frac{E^2 - B^2}{2} & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & \frac{E^2 - B^2}{2} \end{pmatrix} \] Hence \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{E^2 + B^2}{2} & E_y B_z - E_z B_y & E_z B_x - E_x B_z & E_x B_y - E_y B_x \\ E_y B_z - E_z B_y & -E_x^2 - B_x^2 + \frac{1}{2}(E^2 + B^2) & -E_x E_y - B_x B_y & -E_x E_z - B_x B_z \\ E_z B_x - E_x B_z & -E_x E_y - B_x B_y & -E_y^2 - B_y^2 + \frac{1}{2}(E^2 + B^2) & -E_y E_z - B_y B_z \\ E_x B_y - E_y B_x & -E_x E_z - B_x B_z & -E_y E_z - B_y B_z & -E_z^2 - B_z^2 + \frac{1}{2}(E^2 + B^2) \end{pmatrix}, \] which, on multiplying through by \(\mu_0^{-1}\) and dividing each \(\boldsymbol{E}\) field component by \(c\), gives \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} W & \boldsymbol{S}^{\rm T}/c \\ \boldsymbol{S}/c & -\boldsymbol{\sigma} \end{pmatrix}, \] as claimed.

  2. Taking the expression \[ \partial_\nu T^{\mu\nu} = - f^\mu. \] The charged matter 4-current is given as \[ j_\tau = \begin{pmatrix} \rho c\\ -\boldsymbol{j} \end{pmatrix} \] then \[ \begin{align} f^\mu &= F^{\mu\tau} j_\tau &= \begin{pmatrix} (-\boldsymbol{E}/c)\cdot(-\boldsymbol{j}) \\ E_x \rho + B_z j_y - B_y j_z \\ E_y \rho + B_x j_z - B_z j_x \\ E_z \rho + B_y j_x - B_x j_y \end{pmatrix}, &= \begin{pmatrix} \frac{\boldsymbol{E}\cdot \boldsymbol{j}}{c} \\ \rho \boldsymbol{E} + \boldsymbol{j} \times \boldsymbol{B} \end{pmatrix}. \end{align} \] So if \[ \partial_\nu T^{0\nu} = -f^0, \] then \[ c^{-1} \dot{E} + c^{-1} \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot \boldsymbol{S}= - c^{-1} \boldsymbol{j}\cdot \boldsymbol{E}, \] i.e. \[ \dot{E} + \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot \boldsymbol{S} = - \boldsymbol{j}\cdot \boldsymbol{E}, \] as expected. Similarly, for the momentum for each \(i=1,2,3\) we have \[ \partial_\nu T^{i\nu} = - f^i, \] then \[ c^{-2} \dot{S}_i - \partial_k \sigma^{ik} = - \left(\rho E_i + (\boldsymbol{j}\times \boldsymbol{B})_i\right), \] i.e. \[ c^{-2} \dot{\boldsymbol{S}} - \boldsymbol{\nabla}\cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} = - \rho \boldsymbol{E} - \boldsymbol{j}\times \boldsymbol{B}, \] as required. You should recognise the first equation as the conservation of electromagnetic energy. The second equation, which expresses the back-reaction of the Lorentz force on the electromagnetic field, can be interpreted as conservation of electromagnetic momentum.

13.5 First principles construction of field theories

In this course we have constructed field theories from Lorentz scalar and now 4-vector fields. The Lagrangians we have encountered had very similar forms suggesting that there maybe deeper principle constraining their structure. In fact we can postulate three fundamental properties we expect from a theory built from a field \(X\) which could a scalar, 4-vector or tensor:

  1. The Lagrangian \(\mathcal{L}\) governing \(X\) must be a real-valued Lorentz scalar field. This means \(\mathcal{L}\) can only contain terms that are Lorentz scalars formed from contractions of \(X\) and its derivatives \(\partial_\mu X\) using only the metric tensor \(\eta^{\mu\nu}\) and the totally antisymmetric symbol \(\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}\).

  2. We require that field equations derived from \(\mathcal{L}\) be linear equations in \(X\). This means \(\mathcal{L}\) can contain at most terms that are quadratic in \(X\) and \(\partial_\mu X\).

  3. For a given field \(X\) we require \(\mathcal{L}\) to be a theory described exclusively by the type of object the field \(X\) is so there is no hidden dependence on a simpler field object. Enforcing this is equivalent to a form of gauge invariance for \(X\).

As an example we can apply this approach for \(X = \varphi\) is a real Lorentz scalar field. In this case properties 1. and 2. give a general Lagrangian \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm s} = a_1 \varphi + a_2 \varphi^2 + a_3 \partial_\mu\varphi\partial^\mu \varphi, \] where \(a_1,a_2,a_3\) are Lorentz scalar coefficients. Property 3. demands that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm s}\) is invariant to the field transformation \(\varphi \mapsto \varphi + g\), where \(g\) is an arbitrary Lorentz scalar constant. It is easy to show this invariance requires \(a_1 = a_2 = 0\) giving the d’Alembert Lagrangian as the unique scalar field theory up to an overall constant.

The mock exam paper has a section C problem on the content of this section. Some of the statements below are question in this problem.

A much less trivial example, relevant to this lecture, is when \(X = A^\mu\), a real-valued 4-vector field, much like electromagnetism. In this case the application of properties 1. and 2. give a general Lagrangian with numerous terms \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}_{\rm v} &= b_1 A_\mu A^\mu + b_2 \partial_\mu A^\mu + b_3 (\partial_\mu A^\mu) (\partial_\nu A^\nu)\\ &\quad + \, b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) (\partial^\mu A^\nu) + b_5 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) (\partial^\nu A^\mu) \\ &\quad + \, b_6 \epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}(\partial_\mu A_\nu) (\partial_\sigma A_\tau) + b_7 \epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\tau}(\partial_\mu\partial_\nu A_\sigma) A_\tau, \end{align} \tag{13.26}\] where \(b_1,b_2,\cdots,b_7\) are Lorentz scalar coefficients. Notice that the fourth and fifth terms look like those in \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm EM}\) we considered above. However, the sixth term here is the form of term we would have got instead from the second Lorentz invariant for EM. One might ask why we couldn’t have just used this quantity for the EM Lagrangian.

To proceed we simplify this \(\mathcal{L}_v\) by dropping the 2nd term \(\partial_\mu A^\mu\) since this is a 4-divergence term. The seventh term in Equation 13.26 can be shown to be identically zero, meaning \(b_7\) can take any value we wish. Using this, the seventh term can be combined with the sixth term to make a 4-divergence, and hence dropped also. This explains why the second EM Lorentz invariant can’t be used as a Lagrangian.

To make further progress we consider the 4-vector field \[ B^\mu = (\partial_\nu A^\mu)A^\nu - A^\mu(\partial_\nu A^\nu). \tag{13.27}\] We can add multiple of \(\partial_\mu B^\mu\) to \(\mathcal{L}_v\) with no effect on the equations of motion. You are invited to show that this can simplify the Lagrangian further.

Work-along exercises

9. Removing the third term

Show that by adding some multiple of \(\partial_\mu B^\mu\) to \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm v}\) the third term can be cancelled out leaving the simplified Lagrangian Equation 13.28.

Solution

9. Removing the third term

Take given 4-vector field \[ B^\mu = (\partial_\nu A^\mu) A^\nu - A^\mu (\partial_\nu A^\nu) \] We can add any multiple of \(\partial_\mu B^\mu\) without changing the field equations. So: \[ \begin{align} \partial_\mu B^\mu &= (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu A^\mu) A^\nu + (\partial_\nu A^\mu)(\partial_\mu A^\nu) \\ &- (\partial_\mu A^\mu)(\partial_\nu A^\nu) - A^\mu (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu A^\nu). \end{align} \] We next spot that the 1st and 4th terms can be made to equal each other by commuting derivatives and relabelling \[ A^\mu (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu A^\nu) = A^\mu (\partial_\nu \partial_\mu A^\nu) = (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu A^\mu) A^\nu, \] meaning they cancel out. So we are left with \[ \partial_\mu B^\mu = (\partial_\nu A^\mu)(\partial_\mu A^\nu) - (\partial_\mu A^\mu)(\partial_\nu A^\nu), \] which equals the fifth minus the third term in \(\mathcal{L}_v\). Consequently, if we add \(b_3 \partial_\mu B^\mu\) then the 3rd term in \(\mathcal{L}_v\) is cancelled out leaving \[ \mathcal{L}_v = b_1 A_\mu A^\mu + b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu A^\nu) + (b_5 + b_3)(\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\nu A^\mu), \] which equals Equation 13.28 once \((b_5 + b_3) \mapsto b'_5\).

By cancelling out the third term we are left with only three terms \[ \begin{align*} \mathcal{L}_{\rm v} &= b_1 A_\mu A^\mu + b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) (\partial^\mu A^\nu) + b'_5 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) (\partial^\nu A^\mu), \end{align*} \tag{13.28}\] once we redefine the coefficient \((b_5 + b_3) \mapsto b'_5\).

We now come to the application of property 3. to \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm v}\). In this context we require our theory to describe a genuine 4-vector field and have no hidden dependence on a simpler scalar field \(\chi\) that could generate a 4-vector field as \(\partial^\mu\chi\). It thus demands that \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm v}\) is invariant to the field transformation \[ A^\mu \mapsto A^\mu + \partial^\mu \chi, \] meaning that if \(A^\mu\) was a scalar field it could just be gauged away. From this perspective the familiar gauge transformation from electromagnetism is viewed as ensuring that a 4-vector field really is the fundamental description of its physics! Applying this invariance to \(\mathcal{L}_{\rm v}\) results in \(b_1 = 0\) and \(b_4 = - b'_5\) leaving \[ \mathcal{L}_{\rm v} = b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) F^{\mu\nu}, \tag{13.29}\] where \(F^{\mu\nu}\) is the Faraday tensor, as you are invited to now show.

Work-along exercises

10. Final simplification

Show the gauge invariance of property 3. requires that \(b_1 = 0\) and \(b_4 = - b'_5\) resulting in the unique 4-vector theory given in Equation 13.29.

Solution

10. Final simplification

Insert gauge transformation \[ A^\mu \rightarrow A^\mu + \partial^\mu \chi, \] into \(\mathcal{L}_v\) to get \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}_v \rightarrow \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_v &= b_1 (A_\mu + \partial_\mu \chi)(A^\mu + \partial^\mu \chi) + b_4 (\partial_\mu (A_\nu + \partial_\nu \chi))(\partial^\mu (A^\nu + \partial^\nu \chi)) \\ & \quad + \, b_5' (\partial_\mu (A_\nu + \partial_\nu \chi))(\partial^\nu (A^\mu + \partial^\mu \chi)). \end{align} \] Now expand and simplify terms \[ \begin{align} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_v &= b_1 A_\mu A^\mu + 2b_1 A_\mu \partial^\mu \chi + b_1 (\partial_\mu \chi)(\partial^\mu \chi) \\ &\quad + \, b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu A^\nu) + b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu \partial^\nu \chi) \\ &\quad + \, b_4 (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\mu A^\nu) + b_4 (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\mu \partial^\nu \chi) \\ &\quad + \, b_5' (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\nu A^\mu) + b_5' (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\nu \partial^\mu \chi)\\ &\quad + \, b_5' (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\nu A^\mu) + b_5' (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\nu \partial^\mu \chi). \end{align} \] Commute derivatives and relabel some terms: \[ \begin{align} \tilde{\mathcal{L}}_v &= \mathcal{L}_v + 2b_1 A_\mu \partial^\mu \chi + b_1 (\partial_\mu \chi)(\partial^\mu \chi) \\ &\quad + \, 2b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu \partial^\nu \chi) + b_4 (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\mu \partial^\nu \chi) \\ &\quad + \, 2b_5' (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\nu \partial^\mu \chi) + b_5' (\partial_\mu \partial_\nu \chi)(\partial^\nu \partial^\mu \chi). \end{align} \] We then see that picking \(b_5' = - b_4\) cancels out the last 4 terms. However, the first two \(\chi\) dependent terms cannot be cancelled so we are forced to conclude \(b_1 = 0\). This leaves \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}_v &= b_4 \left[(\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu A^\nu) - (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\nu A^\mu)\right], \\ &= b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu)(\partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu), \\ &= b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu) F^{\mu\nu}, \end{align} \] which is Equation 13.29 as required.

The Lagrangian we have ended up with doesn’t look gauge invariant. However, we can easily manipulate it an explicitly gauge invariant form by spliting the term: \[ \mathcal{L}_v = \frac{1}{2} b_4 \left[ (\partial_\mu A_\nu) F^{\mu\nu} + (\partial_\nu A_\mu) F^{\nu\mu}\right]. \] We then use antisymmetry of \(F^{\mu\nu}\) \[ \begin{align} \mathcal{L}_v &= \frac{1}{2} b_4 \left[(\partial_\mu A_\nu) F^{\mu\nu} - (\partial_\nu A_\mu) F^{\mu\nu} \right], \\ &= \frac{1}{2} b_4 (\partial_\mu A_\nu - \partial_\nu A_\mu) F^{\mu\nu}, \end{align} \] and finally before some relabelling to get \[ \mathcal{L}_v = \frac{1}{2} b_4 F_{\mu\nu} F^{\mu\nu}. \] Thus, we have discovered the Lagrangian for a free electromagnetic field as a unique consequence of the three properties.

Curiously, applying the same three properties to a symmetric rank-2 tensor field \(h_{\mu\nu}\) the same reasoning (but with substantially more work) turns out to give a unique Lagrangian that is equivalent to the linearised version of General Relativity!
