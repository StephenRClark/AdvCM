Example Class 3

In this example class we will explore some novel features of relativistic kinematics.

We begin with a Multiple choice quiz.

Example exercise

1. Minkowski’s equation

Given a particle with 4-velocity \(u^\mu\) the 4-momentum is defined \(p^{\mu} \equiv m c u^{\mu} = (\mathcal{E}/c,\boldsymbol{p})\), where \(\mathcal{E}\) is the relativistic energy and \(\boldsymbol{p}\) is the relativistic 3-momentum. Using this the 4-force is defined \[ f^{\mu} \equiv c \frac{{\rm d} p^{\mu}}{{\rm d} s}. \]

  1. Show that Minkowski’s equation of motion applies, \[ f^{\mu} u_{\mu} = 0. \]

Suppose we write \(f^{\mu} = \gamma(g, \boldsymbol{f})\) where \(g\) is a scalar and \(\boldsymbol{f}\) is a 3-vector.

  1. Write down an expression for \(\boldsymbol{f}\) in terms of \(\boldsymbol{p},\) and find an expression for \(g\) in terms of \(\boldsymbol{f}\) using the Minkowski equation.

  2. Does \(g\) agree with what you expect from the original definition of \(f^{\mu}\)?

  3. Can you explain why the Minkowski equation looks like the equation of circular motion in regular 3-vector mechanics?

Solution

1. Minkowski’s equation

  1. Taking the definition given \[ f^\mu = c\,\frac{{\rm d}p^\mu}{{\rm d}s} = mc^2\frac{{\rm d}u^\mu}{{\rm d}s}, \] the left-hand side of the Minkowski equation gives \[ f^\mu u_\mu = mc^2\frac{{\rm d}u^\mu}{{\rm d}s}u_\mu. \] Symmetrising we get \[ f^\mu u_\mu = \frac{1}{2}mc^2\left(\frac{{\rm d}u^\mu}{{\rm d}s}u_\mu + u^\mu\frac{{\rm d}u_\mu}{{\rm d}s}\right), \] hence identifying the product rule derivative we have \[ f^\mu u_\mu = \frac{1}{2}mc^2\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}s}(u_\mu u^\mu). \] However, since \[ u_\mu u^\mu = 1, \] is constant then the derivative is \[ \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}(u_\mu u^\mu)=0, \] and therefore we find Minkowski’s equation \[ f^\mu u_\mu = 0. \]

  2. We take the following form for the 4-force \[ f^\mu = \gamma\begin{pmatrix} g \\ \boldsymbol{f} \end{pmatrix}, \] and the usual form of the 4-momentum \[ p^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} \mathcal{E}/c \\ \boldsymbol{p} \end{pmatrix}, \] which are related as \[ f^\mu = c\frac{{\rm d}p^\mu}{{\rm d}s}. \] Thus the 3-force part of the 4-force is \[ \boldsymbol{f} = \gamma^{-1}c\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}}{{\rm d}s} = \gamma^{-1}c\frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}s}\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}}{{\rm d}t}. \] Since time in our frame and the proper time are related as \[ \frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}s}=\frac{\gamma}{c}, \] we obtain \[ \boldsymbol{f}=\frac{{\rm d}\boldsymbol{p}}{{\rm d}t}, \] showing that \(\boldsymbol{f}\) is the relativistic 3-force since it is the derivative of the relativistic 3-momentum in a particular frame. Given also that \[ f^\mu u_\mu = 0, \] then this constraint becomes \[ \gamma^2(g-\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}/c)=0, \] from which we extract that \[ g = \frac{\boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}. \]

  3. Equating the time-component of \[ f^\mu = c\frac{{\rm d}p^\mu}{{\rm d}s}. \] give \[ g = \gamma^{-1}\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}}{{\rm d}s} = \frac{1}{c}\frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}}{{\rm d}t}, \] which is the rate of change of energy in our frame. The rate of change of energy is the rate at which work is done which is \[ \boldsymbol{f}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} = \frac{{\rm d}\mathcal{E}}{{\rm d}t}, \] so \(g\) agrees with our physical expectations.

  4. For circular motion in regular mechanics the force always acts perpendicular to the velocity and therefore does no work on the particle. The motion has a changing velocity 3-vector but its speed remains constant. In relativity Minkowski’s equation \(f^\mu u_\mu = 0\) has the same form, but now encodes a fundamental constraint that the 4-force cannot change the magnitude of the 4-velocity.

Example exercise

2. Relativistic particle Lagrangian

Here we explore generalizing the Lagrangian approach to relativistic particle mechanics. Specifically, we seek a Lagrangian \(L = L(x^{\mu},\dot{x}^{\mu})\) such that the Euler-Lagrange equations \[\frac{\partial L}{\partial x^j} - \frac{\rm d}{{\rm d} t}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}^j} = 0, \qquad j = 1,2,3,\] where \(\dot{\bullet} = \partial_t\bullet,\) gives us the relativistic equation of motion \(f^j = m a^j\). Note, that we do not necessarily expect the relativistic Lagrangian to have the form `KE \(-\) PE’ any more, since in relativity, energies occur as components of 4-momentum, but the Lagrangian is not a component of a 4-vector. We will assume that the action \[ \mathcal{S} \equiv \int_{t_0}^{t_1}{\rm d}t\, L, \] is Lorentz invariant.

  1. Show that with this assumption, \(\gamma L\) is a Lorentz invariant on the particle’s worldline.

We want to determine the Lagrangian for a free particle, which experiences inertial motion with \(a^{\mu} = 0,\) and we assume, by symmetry, that \(\partial L/\partial x^{\mu} = 0\), meaning there are no spatially dependent fields acting on the particle.

  1. Show that choosing \(L = m \gamma c^2\), the relativistic kinetic energy, does not give the required dependence.

  2. Show that instead \(L_{\mathrm{free}} \equiv -m c^2/\gamma\) is an appropriate choice for the free-particle Lagrangian by confirming its canonical momentum gives the relativistic 3-momentum \(\partial L/\partial \dot{x}^{j} = p^{j}\).

  3. Use the Hamiltonian generated by \(L_{\mathrm{free}}\) to show it is consistent with the relativistic energy \(\mathcal{E} = \gamma mc^2\).

  4. Confirm \(L_{\mathrm{free}}\) does indeed give a Lorentz scalar action.

Solution

2. Relativistic particle Lagrangian

  1. The action is \[ \mathcal{S} = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}{\rm d}t\,L, \] which for the worldline between events \(t_0\) and \(t_1\) is Lorentz invariant. We can change the frame in which we work at each time \(t\) between \(t_0\) and \(t_1\). For this reason we can use the proper time \(s\), which is the time as measured in a momentarily co-moving reference frame with the particle, as a parameter. Then \[ {\rm d}t = \frac{{\rm d}t}{{\rm d}s}{\rm d}s=\frac{\gamma}{c}{\rm d}s. \] The initial and final events on the worldline are labelled as \(s_0\) and \(s_1\), so \[ \mathcal{S} = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}{\rm d}t\,L, = \int_{s_0}^{s_1}{\rm d}s\,\frac{\gamma}{c}L. \] Since \(S\) and \({\rm d}s\) are Lorentz invariant then \(\gamma L\) must also be Lorentz invariant.

  2. Try \(L = \gamma mc^2\). We then compute the contributions to the Euler-Lagrange equations \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i} = 0, \] and \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}^i} = \frac{\partial}{\partial v^i} \left[ mc^2(1-v^2/c^2)^{-1/2}\right] = \gamma^3 m v^i. \] Together these give \[ \frac{d}{dt}(\gamma^3 m v^i)=0. \] However, for a free particle the 4-force is zero \[ f^\mu = mc^2\frac{{\rm d}u^\mu}{{\rm d}s} = 0 \] so for the spatial components of \(U^\mu\) we have \[ \frac{{\rm d}u^j}{{\rm d}s} = \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}s}(\gamma v^j) = \frac{\gamma}{c}\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}(\gamma v^j) = 0 \] hence multiplying by \(m\) we instead have \[ \frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}t}(\gamma m v^j) = \frac{{\rm d}p^j}{{\rm d}t} = 0, \] giving that the relativistic 3-momentum does not change with time. In contrast \(L = \gamma mc^2\) does not produce this result.

  3. Instead choose \[ L=-\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}. \] Again computing the Euler-Lagrange contributions gives \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial x^i}=0, \] and \[ \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}^i} =\frac{\partial}{\partial v^i}\left(-mc^2\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\right) = m\gamma v^i. \] This confirms that the canonical momentum of this Lagrangian is indeed the relativistic 3-momentum \(p^i\). Moreover the Euler–Lagrange equation now yields \[ \frac{d}{dt}(m\gamma v^i) = 0, \qquad i=1,2,3, \] as required for a free relativistic particle.

  4. Use the standard relation \(L = \dot{x}^i p_i - H\) connecting the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian, and then compute \[ H = \dot{x}^i p_i - L = \gamma m v^i v_i + \frac{mc^2}{\gamma} = \gamma mc^2\left(\frac{v^2}{c^2} + \frac{1}{\gamma^2}\right) = \gamma mc^2, \] hence we recover the relativistic energy as required.

  5. Inserting \(L_{\mathrm{free}} \equiv -m c^2/\gamma\) into the action gives \[ \mathcal{S} = -\int_{s_0}^{s_1}{\rm d}s\, \frac{\gamma}{c}\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} = -mc\int_{s_0}^{s_1}{\rm d}s. \] Since \(m\), \(c\) and the integral over the proper time are all Lorentz invariant we confirm that the action \(\mathcal{S}\) is Lorentz invariant, as expected. Moreover, as the action is the integral over the proper time along the worldline this shows that the trajectory of the particle will be the one which minimises the space-time interval between its endpoints – it is a geodesic. Altogether our workings above confirm that despite looking weird is the correct Lagrangian for a relativistic particle.

Example exercise

3. Interacting Lagrangian for relativistic particle

Any “potential energy” \(\Phi\) in the Lagrangian should properly be considered as the 0-component of a “4-potential” \(\phi^{\mu} = (\Phi,\boldsymbol{\phi}).\) The 3-vector \(\boldsymbol{\phi}\) is a `potential momentum’ not usually present in classical physics. Thus we might expect the 3-scalar potential \(\Phi\), in the relativistic Lagrangian, to be replaced by a term such as \(\phi^{\mu} u_{\mu}/\gamma.\) Our relativistic Lagrangian is therefore \(L = -mc^2/\gamma - \phi^{\mu} u_{\mu}/\gamma.\)

  1. Show that, when \(v \ll c,\) this is equivalent to the familiar non-relativistic Lagrangian.

  2. Find the 1,2,3-components of the canonical relativistic momentum \(p^j\); what do you notice?

  3. The relativistic energy (0-component of the canonical momentum \(p^0\)) is provided by the relativistic Hamiltonian \(H = \sum_{j=1}^3 p^j \dot{x}^j - L.\) Compute \(H.\)

Solution

3. Interacting Lagrangian for relativistic particle

  1. Consider the relativistic Lagrangian for a particle interacting with a 4-potential \[ L = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} - \frac{\phi^\mu u_\mu}{\gamma}, \] where the Lorentz factor is the standard \[ \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}}. \] In the non-relativistic limit \[ v \ll c, \] we expand
    \[ \gamma^{-1} = \left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{1/2} \approx 1 - \frac{v^2}{2c^2}. \] Thus the free particle contribution becomes \[ L_{\text{free}} = -\frac{mc^2}{\gamma} \approx -mc^2\left(1-\frac{v^2}{2c^2}\right), \] and hence \[ L_{\text{free}} \approx -mc^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2, \] to first order in \((v/c)^2\). We therefore recover the non-relativistic kinetic energy minus the relativistic rest energy. For the interaction term we write the 4-potential as \[ \phi^\mu = \begin{pmatrix}\Phi \\ \boldsymbol{\phi} \end{pmatrix}, \] and the 4-velocity \[ u^\mu = \gamma \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \boldsymbol{v}/c \end{pmatrix}. \] Then we have \[ \phi^\mu u_\mu = \gamma(\Phi - \boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}/c), \] so dividing by \(\gamma\) gives this term as \[ \frac{\phi^\mu u_\mu}{\gamma} = \Phi - \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}, \] in any limit. The full Lagrangian in the non-relativistic limit becomes \[ L \approx -mc^2 + \frac{1}{2}mv^2 - \Phi + \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}. \] The constant term \(mc^2\) does not contribute to the Euler–Lagrange equations, so it may be ignored. Therefore we recover the Newtonian structure \(L = \text{KE} - \text{PE}\), but have acquired an additional velocity-dependent interaction term which represents the magnetic contribution.

  2. The canonical momentum is defined as \[ p_j = \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{x}_j} = \frac{\partial L}{\partial v_j}. \] Starting from the relativistic Lagrangian \[ L = -mc^2\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}} - \left(\Phi - \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}\right), \] we compute the derivative of the kinetic term as \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial v_j}\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}} = -\frac{v_j}{c^2}\left(1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right)^{-1/2}, \] to obtain \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial v_j}\left[-mc^2\sqrt{1-\frac{v^2}{c^2}}\right] = m\gamma v_j, \] which is the relativistic 3-momentum as expected from question 2.. However, here the interaction term gives \[ \frac{\partial}{\partial v_j} \left(\frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}\right) = \frac{\phi_j}{c}, \] so the canonical momentum now becomes \[ p_j = m\gamma v_j + \frac{\phi_j}{c}. \] This has the same structure as the canonical momentum appearing in electromagnetism (reviewed later in Review on Electromagnetism), \[ \boldsymbol{p} = m\gamma \boldsymbol{v} + q\boldsymbol{A}, \] if we identify from the non-relativistic Lagrangian for a charged particle in an EM field as \[ \Phi = qV, \] and \[ \boldsymbol{\phi} = qc\,\boldsymbol{A}. \] The gives us a strong indication of how interactions of matter fields with electromagnetic interactions may be accounted for in their Lagrangian. We will use this result later in the course.

  3. As stated the Hamiltonian is defined by \[ H = \sum_{i=1}^{3} p_i \dot{x}_i - L, \] and substituting the canonical momentum gives \[ H = \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} - L. \] Using \[ \boldsymbol{p} = m\gamma\boldsymbol{v} + \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}}{c}, \] we obtain \[ \boldsymbol{p}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} = m\gamma v^2 + \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}. \] Hence \[ H = m\gamma v^2 + \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c} + \frac{mc^2}{\gamma} + \Phi - \frac{\boldsymbol{\phi}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}}{c}. \] The interaction terms cancel, leaving \[ H = mc^2\gamma + \Phi. \] Thus the Hamiltonian is the sum of relativistic energy and potential energy \[ H = E_{\text{rel}} + \Phi. \]

The relativistic Lagrangian we have found here, \(L = -mc^2/\gamma - \phi^{\mu}u_{\mu}/\gamma,\) has two terms; the first term was chosen to give the correct equation of motion for a free-particle (see question 2.). The second term, representing an interaction with some external potential (whose gradient gives the force), couples the potential with the 4-velocity, the particle’s key dynamical variable. The Lagrangian densities for fields we will investigate later will also have this general character. The treatment will also be symmetric in space and time, unlike the Euler-Lagrange equations here.

We have thus shown that the form of the Lorentz force Lagrangian, complete with the vector potential and the 3-velocity, arises very naturally from relativistic considerations; the Lorentz force arises from \(\phi^{\mu} = q c A^{\mu}\), where \(A^\mu\) is the 4-potential defined later in Lagrangian approach to EM. We will investigate this further when we derive the relativistic Lorentz equation of motion from the relativistic Lorentz force Lagrangian with this form. A starting point for this is already apparent in the next question.

Example exercise

4. A 4-velocity dependent 4-force

In general, a force exerted on a particle can depend on its 4-velocity as well as its position. The simplest such covariant dependence is linear, occurring for a 4-force given by \[ f^{\mu} = T^{\mu \nu} u_{\nu}, \] for some second-rank tensor \(T^{\mu\nu}.\) We can split \(T^{\mu \nu}\) into its symmetric part \[ S^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}(T^{\mu \nu} + T^{\nu \mu}), \] and antisymmetric part \[ A^{\mu\nu} = \frac{1}{2}(T^{\mu \nu} - T^{\nu \mu}). \]

  1. Show that the antisymmetric part satisfies Minkowski’s equation \(A^{\mu\nu}u_{\mu}u_{\nu} = 0\).

  2. By considering different choices of \(u^{\mu}\), show that the only way the symmetric part can satisfy Minkowski’s equation \(S^{\mu\nu}u_{\mu}u_{\nu} = 0\) is for all components of \(S^{\mu\nu}\) to be zero.

  3. If \(T^{\mu\nu}\) is given by \[ T^{\mu\nu} = \left( \begin{array}{cccc} 0 & -e_x & -e_y & -e_z \\ e_x & 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ e_y & b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ e_z & -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{array}\right), \] find an expression for \(\boldsymbol{f}\) in terms of 3-vectors \(\boldsymbol{e}\) and \(\boldsymbol{b}.\) Does this expression look familiar?

Solution

4. A 4-velocity dependent 4-force

  1. If we take the 4-force with the form \[ f^\mu = T^{\mu\nu}u_\nu, \] then from the Minkowski equation, \[ f^\mu U_\mu = T^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = 0 \] The 4-velocity here is arbitrary, any particle travelling with any \(u^\mu\) experiencing \(f^\mu\) will satisfy this equation. We decompose \(T^{\mu\nu}\) into its symmetric and antisymmetric parts \[ T^{\mu\nu} = S^{\mu\nu} + A^{\mu\nu}, \] with \[ S^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(T^{\mu\nu}+T^{\nu\mu}), \] \[ A^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}(T^{\mu\nu}-T^{\nu\mu}). \] where \[ S^{\mu\nu} = S^{\nu\mu}, \qquad A^{\mu\nu} = -A^{\nu\mu}. \] Now for the antisymmetric part \[ A^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = -A^{\mu\nu}u_\nu u_\mu = -A^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu, \] hence \[ A^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = 0, \] as required.

  2. If \(T^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = 0\) we need to show that \[ S^{\mu\nu}u_\mu u_\nu = 0, \tag{1}\] for any \(u^\mu\). Since \(S^{\mu\nu}\) is, by assumption, independent of \(u^\mu\) its elements in a given frame are fixed. We therefore pick various \(u^\mu\) and demonstrate that Equation 1 various elements of \(S^{\mu\nu}\) have to be zero. For example, we start by choosing \[ u^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \boldsymbol{0} \end{pmatrix}, \] hence Equation 1 reduces to \(S^{00} = 0\), establishing this element vanishes. Next we choose a pair of 4-velocities along the \(x\)-axis with equal and opposite velocities (so identical \(\gamma\) factors): \[ u^\mu_\pm = \gamma\begin{pmatrix} 1 \\ \pm (v/c)\boldsymbol{e}_1 \end{pmatrix}, \] which gives two equations \[ S^{\mu\nu}u^\mu_\pm u^\nu_\pm = \gamma^2\left(\pm\frac{v}{c}(S^{10} + S^{01})+S^{11}\frac{v^2}{c^2}\right) = 0 \] Taking the sum and the difference of these two equations then gives \[ S^{11} = 0, \quad {\rm and} \quad (S^{10} + S^{01}) = 0 \implies S^{10} = S^{01} = 0. \] Choosing new pairs along the \(y\)-axis similarly shows that \(S^{22} = 0\) along with \(S^{20} = S^{02} = 0\), and the \(z\)-axis to show that \(S^{33} = 0\) and \(S^{30} = S^{03} = 0\). Pickng 4-velocities orientated along two spatial axes can then eliminate the remaining 3 elements showing all \(S^{\mu\nu} = 0\) for Equation 1 to be satisfied for all \(u^\mu\). Hence we conclude that \[ T^{\mu\nu} = A^{\mu\nu}, \] so \(T^{\mu\nu}\) must be antisymmetric.

  3. Let \(T^{\mu\nu}\) be built from two 3-vectors \(\boldsymbol{e}\) and \(\boldsymbol{b}\) as \[ T^{\mu\nu}= \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -e_x & -e_y & -e_z \\ e_x & 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ e_y & b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ e_z & -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}, \] with \[ f^\mu=T^{\mu\nu}u_\nu, \qquad u^\nu=\gamma \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ \boldsymbol{v}/c \end{pmatrix} \qquad {\rm hence} \qquad u_\nu=\gamma \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -\boldsymbol{v}/c \end{pmatrix} \] We can evaluate \(f^\mu\) directly by matrix-vector multiplication: \[ f^\mu = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & -e_x & -e_y & -e_z \\ e_x & 0 & -b_z & b_y \\ e_y & b_z & 0 & -b_x \\ e_z & -b_y & b_x & 0 \end{pmatrix} \gamma \begin{pmatrix} 1\\ -v_x/c \\ -v_y/c \\ -v_z/c \end{pmatrix} = \gamma \begin{pmatrix} \boldsymbol{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{v} \\ e_x + b_zv_y/c - b_yv_z/c \\ e_y -b_zv_x/c + b_xv_z/c \\ e_z + b_yv_x/c - b_xv_y/c\end{pmatrix}. \] Compared to question 1. we note that the time-component gives \(g = \boldsymbol{e}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}\), which looks like the rate of working being done, while the force 3-vector is \[ \boldsymbol{f} = \boldsymbol{e} + \frac{\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{b}}{c}. \] This part has the same form as the Lorentz force law \[ \boldsymbol{f} = q\boldsymbol{E}+q\frac{\boldsymbol{v}\times\boldsymbol{B}}{c}, \] once we identify: \[ \boldsymbol{e}=q\boldsymbol{E}, \qquad \boldsymbol{b}=qc\boldsymbol{B}. \] This result will be crucial for us to identify the Faraday tensor in a later lecture.
